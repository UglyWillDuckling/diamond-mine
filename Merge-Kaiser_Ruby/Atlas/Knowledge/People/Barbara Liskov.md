**Barbara Jane Liskov** ([née](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_name#Maiden_and_married_names "Birth name") **Huberman**; born November 7, 1939) is an American [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist "Computer scientist") who has made pioneering contributions to [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages "Programming languages") and [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing "Distributed computing"). Her notable work includes the introduction of [abstract data types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_data_type "Abstract data type") and the accompanying principle of [data abstraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstraction_\(computer_science\) "Abstraction (computer science)"), along with the [Liskov substitution principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liskov_substitution_principle "Liskov substitution principle"), which applies these ideas to [object-oriented programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object-oriented_programming "Object-oriented programming"), [subtyping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtyping "Subtyping"), and [inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inheritance_\(object-oriented_programming\) "Inheritance (object-oriented programming)"). Her work was recognized with the 2008 [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award "Turing Award"), the highest distinction in computer science.

Liskov is one of the earliest women to have been granted a doctorate in computer science in the United States, and the second woman to receive the Turing Award. She is currently an [Institute Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Institute_Professors_at_the_Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "List of Institute Professors at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology") and Ford Professor of Engineering at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology "Massachusetts Institute of Technology").[^2] [^3]

## Early life and education

Liskov was born November 7, 1939, in Los Angeles, California,[^4] the eldest of Jane (née Dickhoff) and Moses Huberman's four children.[^5] She earned her bachelor's degree in mathematics with a minor in physics at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley "University of California, Berkeley") in 1961. At Berkeley, she had only one other female classmate in her major.[^6] She applied to graduate mathematics programs at Berkeley and [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University"). At the time, Princeton was not accepting female students in mathematics.[^7] She was accepted at Berkeley but instead moved to Boston and began working at the [MITRE Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitre_Corporation "Mitre Corporation"), where she became interested in computers and programming. She worked at Mitre for one year before taking a programming job at Harvard, where she worked on language translation.[^7]

She then decided to go back to school and applied again to Berkeley, but also to Stanford and Harvard. In March 1968 she became [one of the first women](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women_in_computing "Women in computing") in the United States to be awarded a Ph.D. from a computer science department at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University "Stanford University").[^8] [^9] [^10] At Stanford, she worked with [John McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCarthy_\(computer_scientist\) "John McCarthy (computer scientist)") and was supported in her work in [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence").[^7] The topic of her Ph.D. thesis was a computer program to play [chess endgames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_endgame "Chess endgame"), for which she developed the important [killer heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_heuristic "Killer heuristic").[^11]

## Career

After graduating from Stanford, Liskov returned to Mitre to work as a research staff.[^2]

Liskov has led many significant projects, including the Venus operating system, a small, low-cost [time-sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-sharing "Time-sharing") system; the design and implementation of [CLU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CLU_\(programming_language\) "CLU (programming language)"); [Argus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argus_\(programming_language\) "Argus (programming language)"), the first high-level language to support the implementation of distributed programs and to demonstrate the technique of [promise pipelining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Promise_pipelining "Promise pipelining"); and Thor, an [object-oriented database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_database "Object database") system. With [Jeannette Wing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeannette_Wing "Jeannette Wing"), she developed a particular definition of [subtyping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtyping "Subtyping"), commonly known as the [Liskov substitution principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liskov_substitution_principle "Liskov substitution principle"). She leads the Programming Methodology Group at [MIT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT "MIT"), with a current research focus in [Byzantine fault tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_fault_tolerance "Byzantine fault tolerance") and [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing "Distributed computing").[^3] She was on the inaugural Engineering and Computer Science jury for the [Infosys Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infosys_Prize "Infosys Prize") in 2009.[^12] Liskov's design and development of CLU and Argus would later have influence on many well-known programming languages such as Java, C++, C#, and Ada.[^13]

Liskov’s formulation of the Liskov substitution principle (LSP) has had a lasting influence on software engineering theory and practice. The principle became a foundational concept in software engineering education, where it is widely taught as a formal criterion for subtype correctness in object-oriented design. It is commonly presented in university curricula and textbooks as a key rule for reasoning about behavioral compatibility in programs.[^14]

## Recognition and awards

Liskov is a member of the [National Academy of Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Engineering "National Academy of Engineering"), the [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences "National Academy of Sciences"), and a fellow of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences "American Academy of Arts and Sciences") and of the [Association for Computing Machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery "Association for Computing Machinery") (ACM). In 2002, she was recognized as one of the top women faculty members at MIT, and among the top 50 faculty members in the sciences in the U.S.[^15] In 2002, *[Discover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discover_\(magazine\) "Discover (magazine)")* magazine recognized Liskov as one of the 50 most important women in science.[^16]

In 2004, Barbara Liskov won the [John von Neumann Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Medal "John von Neumann Medal") for "fundamental contributions to programming languages, programming methodology, and [distributed systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing "Distributed computing") ".[^17] On 19 November, 2005, Barbara Liskov and [Donald E. Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_E._Knuth "Donald E. Knuth") were awarded [ETH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH "ETH") Honorary Doctorates.[^18] Liskov and Knuth were also featured in the ETH Zurich Distinguished Colloquium Series.[^19] She was awarded a [Doctorate Honoris Causa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate_Honoris_Causa "Doctorate Honoris Causa") by the [University of Lugano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universit%C3%A0_della_Svizzera_Italiana "Università della Svizzera Italiana") in 2011 [^20] and by [Universidad Politécnica de Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universidad_Polit%C3%A9cnica_de_Madrid "Universidad Politécnica de Madrid") in 2018.[^21]

Liskov received the 2008 [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award "Turing Award") from the ACM in March 2009,[^22] for her work in the design of programming languages and software methodology that led to the development of [object-oriented programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object-oriented_programming "Object-oriented programming").[^23] Specifically, Liskov developed two programming languages, [CLU](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CLU_\(programming_language\) "CLU (programming language)") [^24] in the 1970s and [Argus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argus_\(programming_language\) "Argus (programming language)") [^25] in the 1980s.[^23] The ACM cited her contributions to the practical and theoretical foundations of "programming language and [system design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_design "System design"), especially related to [data abstraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstraction_\(computer_science\) "Abstraction (computer science)"), [fault tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fault-tolerant_design "Fault-tolerant design"), and [distributed computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributed_computing "Distributed computing") ".[^26] In 2012, she was inducted into the [National Inventors Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Inventors_Hall_of_Fame "National Inventors Hall of Fame").[^27]

In 2023 Liskov was awarded the Benjamin Franklin Medal from the Franklin Institute for "seminal contributions to computer programming languages and methodology, enabling the implementation of reliable, reusable programs".[^28]

## Personal life

Liskov is Jewish.[^29] In 1970, she married Nathan Liskov.[^7] They have one son, Moses, who earned a Ph.D. in computer science from MIT in 2004 and teaches computer science at the [College of William & Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_of_William_and_Mary "College of William and Mary").[^2]
