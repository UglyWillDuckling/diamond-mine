***Sicanje***, ***bocanje*** or ***bockanje*** was a traditional [tattoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattoo "Tattoo") custom practiced mostly among the [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church "Catholic Church") [Bosnian-Herzegovinian Croat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croats_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Croats of Bosnia and Herzegovina") community, and to a lesser extent in present-day [Croatian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia "Croatia") region of [Dalmatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalmatia "Dalmatia"), mainly by teenage girls, but also boys.

## History

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:On_Wednesday_16th_October_1912_-_French_photographer_Auguste_L%C3%A9on_was_in_Sarajevo,_-Bosnia,_where_he_took_this_autochrome_of_a_tattooed_Catholic_woman.jpg)

Sarajevan Catholic, 1912. By Auguste Léon.

Tattooing of young girls and boys in [Bosnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_\(region\) "Bosnia (region)") and [Herzegovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herzegovina "Herzegovina") is colloquially called *sicanje*, *bocanje* or *bockanje*, and it was a widespread custom mostly among Catholic Croats in the [central regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_Bosnia "Central Bosnia").[^1] Archaeologist [Ćiro Truhelka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C4%86iro_Truhelka "Ćiro Truhelka") researched these types of tattoos in the late 19th century, becoming one of the first to write about them and to illustrate them.[^2] [^3] In 1894, a [Bosnian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_rule_in_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Austro-Hungarian rule in Bosnia and Herzegovina") -based doctor named Leopold Glück published an article in [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna") titled *Die Tätowirung der Haut bei den Katholiken Bosniens und der Herzegowina* (*The Tattooing of Skin Among the Catholics of Bosnia and Herzegovina*) detailing the tattoos observed among the locals.[^4]

Women in some parts of the country tattooed their hands and other visible parts of their bodies (such as the brow, cheeks, wrist, or below the neck) with [Christian symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_symbols "Christian symbols") and *[stećak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ste%C4%87ak "Stećak")* ornaments. Boys were also tattooed with the same symbols mostly above the elbow on the right arm, chest, forehead, and pointer finger. This can be seen today, not only in Bosnia and Herzegovina but among ethnic Croats from Bosnia and Herzegovina [living abroad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatian_diaspora "Croatian diaspora").[^5] [^6] Children were tattooed from as early as the age of six, usually during the period between the feast of [Saint Joseph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Joseph "Saint Joseph") in March to the feast of [Saint John the Baptist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nativity_of_Saint_John_the_Baptist "Nativity of Saint John the Baptist") in June.[^7]

Catholics tattooed their children in order to save them from [devshirme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devshirme "Devshirme"), while women were tattooed in hopes of avoiding [enslavement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery_in_the_Ottoman_Empire "Slavery in the Ottoman Empire") or to avoid [conversion to Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Islamization of Bosnia and Herzegovina") in general.[^7] [^8]

## Geographical extent in Bosnia and Herzegovina and Croatia

The practice of traditional tattooing was recorded in nearly all regions of Bosnia and Herzegovina where Croats live. Exceptions are the areas around [Banja Luka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banja_Luka "Banja Luka"), [Derventa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derventa "Derventa") and parts of southern Herzegovina.[^9]

In Croatia, the practice of traditional tattooing was recorded in some villages of the [Šibenik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%A0ibenik "Šibenik") hinterland, including the municipalities of [Muć](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mu%C4%87 "Muć"), [Kijevo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kijevo,_Croatia "Kijevo, Croatia"), [Unešić](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Une%C5%A1i%C4%87 "Unešić"), [Klis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klis "Klis") and Šibenik itself. It was also recorded in villages in [southern Dalmatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Dalmatia "Southern Dalmatia") bordering Bosnia and Herzegovina in the municipalities of [Ston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ston "Ston"), [Metković](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metkovi%C4%87 "Metković") and in one [Slavonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavonia "Slavonia") village in the municipality of [Osijek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osijek "Osijek").[^10]

## Folk explanation of traditional tattooing

Many explanations for the practice of traditional tattooing among Croats of Bosnia and Herzegovina are recorded. The ethnologist Mario Petrić categorised the explanations into the following groups:

- A sign of Christianity [^11]
- A sign of Catholicism [^11]
- Prevention of [conversions to Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamization_of_Bosnia_and_Herzegovina "Islamization of Bosnia and Herzegovina") [^11] [^12]
- Decoration [^13]
- A sign for endurance of pain [^13]
- Other explanations (e.g.: [Ottoman violence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_in_the_Ottoman_Empire#Persecution "Christianity in the Ottoman Empire"), mimicking [Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus "Jesus") 's [suffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passion_of_Jesus "Passion of Jesus") on [the Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_Cross "True Cross"), mutual recognition of Catholics, etc.) [^13]

Other recorded explanations include the hope of Catholic parents to recognise their children [^14] [^15] who were [forcibly recruited as soldiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devshirme "Devshirme") and other protective functions against forced [^16] and voluntary conversion to Islam.[^17]

## Motifs

![](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Frau_aus_dem_Lasvatale.jpg)

Tattoos on a Catholic woman from the Lašva Valley in central Bosnia.

The most common and widespread symbols tattooed were the cross (*križ*) [^18], bracelet (*narukvica*), fence (*ograda*) and branches or twigs (*grančica*).[^19] [^20]

The cross had numerous variations, with one of the most common ones included small branch-like lines called " *grančica* " or " *jelica* " ([pine tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pine_tree "Pine tree")). Bracelet-like designs were sometimes tattooed around the women's wrists, either with crosses or a fence-like motif. There were many non-Christian, or [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavic_paganism "Slavic paganism") symbols used, the most common consisting of circles believed to be connected to the traditional circle (" *[kolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kolo_\(dance\) "Kolo (dance)")* ") dances of the villages.[^8] The pagan and Christian symbols were mixed together indiscriminately, with the first originating from nature and family in Illyrian times, and the other with later adapted Christian meaning.[^8] [^19] The most common areas to tattoos were the arms and hands (including fingers), and on the chest and forehead.[^21].

## Modern

The custom of tattooing young girls and boys died out after [World War II in Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II_in_Yugoslavia "World War II in Yugoslavia") with the establishment of the [FPR Yugoslavia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_Federal_Republic_of_Yugoslavia "Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia"), and tattoos done by the traditional method are now only seen on old women.[^22] [^23] Today, there is a growing trend of modern tattoo artists utilising the traditional designs with contemporary tattooing methods in Croatia and Bosnia and Herzegovina.[^24]

## In media

In 2013, a documentary titled *Sicanje, bocanje, tetoviranje* aired on Croatian television channel [HRT 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HRT_3 "HRT 3").[^23] [^25] In 2011, *[Vice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vice_Media "Vice Media")* published an article titled "The Croatian Tattooed Grandma Cult" about the phenomena.[^26] Furthermore, *Vice Serbia* released a story and short film titled *Tetovirane bake* (*Tattooed Grandmas*), where they interview various Bosnian Croat women about their tattoos.[^27]

The Croatian girl group [Lelek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lelek_\(band\) "Lelek (band)"), who is set to [represent Croatia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Croatia_in_the_Eurovision_Song_Contest_2026 "Croatia in the Eurovision Song Contest 2026") at the [Eurovision Song Contest 2026](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurovision_Song_Contest_2026 "Eurovision Song Contest 2026") with the song " [Andromeda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andromeda_\(Lelek_song\) "Andromeda (Lelek song)") ", heavily features the tattoos in their styling.[^28]

## Related practices

The practice of tattooing on the [Balkans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balkans "Balkans"), which also has been [present among Albanians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albanian_traditional_tattooing "Albanian traditional tattooing") and also [Vlach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vlach "Vlach") women from [Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece "Greece"), [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_\(region\) "Macedonia (region)").[^29] [^30]

The [Eastern Orthodox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Orthodox_Church "Eastern Orthodox Church") [Slavic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavs "Slavs") population abhorred this practice.[^31] [^32]

In the 1st century BC, the Greek historian [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo "Strabo") wrote of tattooing among inhabitants of this area, namely [Illyrians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illyrians "Illyrians") and [Thracians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thracians "Thracians"),[^33] [^34] along with other customs.[^35]
