***The Lathe of Heaven*** is a 1971 [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction "Science fiction") [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel "Novel") by American writer [Ursula K. Le Guin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_K._Le_Guin "Ursula K. Le Guin"), first serialized in the American science fiction magazine *[Amazing Stories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amazing_Stories "Amazing Stories")*. It won the 1972 [Locus Award for Best Novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locus_Award_for_Best_Novel "Locus Award for Best Novel") [^1] and was a finalist for the 1971 [Nebula Award for Best Novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula_Award_for_Best_Novel "Nebula Award for Best Novel") [^2] and 1972 [Hugo Award for Best Novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hugo_Award_for_Best_Novel "Hugo Award for Best Novel").[^3] Two [television film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Television_film "Television film") adaptations were released: the [PBS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS "PBS") production, *[The Lathe of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lathe_of_Heaven_\(film\) "The Lathe of Heaven (film)")* (1980), and *[Lathe of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lathe_of_Heaven_\(film\) "Lathe of Heaven (film)")* (2002), a remake produced by the [A&E Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26E_Network "A&E Network").

The novel explores themes and philosophies such as [positivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positivism "Positivism"), [Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism "Taoism"), [behaviorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behaviorism "Behaviorism"), and [utilitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism "Utilitarianism"). Its central plot surrounds a man whose dreams are able to alter past and present reality and the ramifications of those psychologically derived changes for better and worse.

The title is from the writings of [Chuang Tzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhuang_Zhou "Zhuang Zhou") (Zhuang Zhou) – specifically a passage from Book XXIII, paragraph 7, quoted as an [epigraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epigraph_\(literature\) "Epigraph (literature)") to Chapter 3 of the novel:

> > 知止乎其所不能知，至矣。若有不即是者，天鈞敗之。
> 
> > To let understanding stop at what cannot be understood is a high attainment. Those who cannot do it will be destroyed on the [lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lathe "Lathe") of heaven.

Other epigraphs from Chuang Tzu appear throughout the novel. Le Guin chose the title because she loved the quotation. However, it seems that quote is a mis-translation of Chuang Tzu's Chinese text.

In *Nothingness, Being, and Dao: Ontology and Cosmology in the Zhuangzi*, Chai describes the concept of 天鈞 as 'heavenly equilibrium'.[^4]

In an interview with [Bill Moyers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Moyers "Bill Moyers") for the 2000 [DVD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DVD "DVD") release of the 1980 adaptation, Le Guin clarified the issue:

> ... it's a terrible mis-translation apparently, I didn't know that at the time. There were no lathes in China at the time that was said. [Joseph Needham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Needham "Joseph Needham") wrote me and said "It's a lovely translation, but it's wrong".[^5]

In 1997, Le Guin published a translation of the *[Tao Te Ching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tao_Te_Ching "Tao Te Ching")* by [Lao Tzu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lao_Tzu "Lao Tzu"), traditionally regarded as the founder of [Taoism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoism "Taoism") (Daoism). In the notes at the end of the translation, she explains her choice of title for the novel:

> The language of some \[translations of the *Tao Te Ching*\] was so obscure as to make me feel the book must be beyond Western comprehension. ([James Legge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Legge "James Legge") 's version was one of these, although I found the title for a book of mine, *The Lathe of Heaven*, in Legge. Years later, Joseph Needham, the great scholar of Chinese science and technology, wrote to tell me in the kindest, most unreproachful fashion Legge was a bit off on that one; when *Chuang Tzu* was written the lathe hadn't been invented.) [^6]

Translated editions of *Lathe of Heaven* titled the novel differently. The German and first Portuguese edition titles, *Die Geißel des Himmels* and *O flagelo dos céus*, mean literally "the scourge \[or whip\] of heaven". The French, Swedish and second Portuguese edition titles, *L'autre côté du rêve*, *På andra sidan drömmen* and *Do outro lado do sonho*, translate as "the other side of the dream". The Italian edition title is *La falce dei cieli* (The scythe of the heavens)

## Plot summary

The book is set in [Portland, Oregon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Portland,_Oregon "Portland, Oregon"), in the year 2002. Portland has three million inhabitants and continuous rain. Many citizens suffer from malnutrition and poverty. The world is seriously affected by war in the [Middle East](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_East "Middle East") and [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change "Climate change").

George Orr is a [draftsman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_drawing "Technical drawing") with a mysterious ability. Some of his [dreams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreams "Dreams") are "effective" dreams that change reality. Orr's effective dreams shift the entire universe, changing both the past and present and creating a new timeline. Only Orr remembers that things were ever different, with the exception of people who watch him directly when the dreams occur. Orr begins abusing drugs to suppress his dreams.

Orr begins attending mandated therapy sessions with ambitious [psychiatrist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychiatrist "Psychiatrist") and sleep researcher Dr. William Haber. After he witnesses Orr's dreams change a picture in his office, Haber believes in Orr's powers. Dr. Haber seeks to change the planet. Haber's experiments with a [biofeedback/EEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brainwave_synchronization "Brainwave synchronization") machine, nicknamed the Augmentor, enhance Orr's abilities while producing a series of increasingly intolerable [alternative worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_universe_\(fiction\) "Parallel universe (fiction)") based on an assortment of [utopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utopia "Utopia") (and [dystopian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dystopia "Dystopia")) premises:

- Eliminating [over-population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_overpopulation "Human overpopulation") is disastrous after Orr dreams that a devastating [plague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epidemic "Epidemic") eliminated most humans.
- Attempting to remove the scourge of [cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cancer "Cancer") from society creates a world where citizens are routinely allowed to euthanize one another for being [considered a threat to the gene pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics "Eugenics").
- "Peace on Earth" results in an alien invasion of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon "Moon"), uniting mankind against the external threat while creating new conflict in [cislunar space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cislunar_space "Cislunar space").

Orr is concerned that Haber is violating his civil rights. He contacts lawyer Heather Lelache, who witnesses one of Haber's therapy sessions. Lelache then gains memories of two different timelines, proving the existence of effective dreaming. Orr tells Lelache that the world was destroyed by [nuclear war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_warfare "Nuclear warfare") in April 1998. Orr dreamed it back into existence as he lay dying in the ruins. He now doubts the reality of what now exists, considering it a mere dream.

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/6c/Portland%26MtHood.jpg/250px-Portland%26MtHood.jpg)

Portland and Mount Hood play a central role in the setting of the novel.

Afraid of the potential harm Haber could do to reality, Orr considers [suicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suicide "Suicide"). Lelache suggests that she could hypnotize Orr and try to trigger an effective dream in which Haber is more benevolent. She also suggests to a dreaming Orr that the aliens should no longer be on the Moon. This triggers an unintentional scenario in which the aliens invade Earth and begin attacking.

During this disaster, Orr returns to Haber, who orders Orr to dream of peaceful aliens. For a time, everybody experiences stability, but Haber continues meddling. His suggestion that Orr dream away [racism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racism "Racism") results in everyone becoming gray, and changes much of history: "he had searched his memory and had found in it no [address that had been delivered on a battlefield in Gettysburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gettysburg_Address "Gettysburg Address"), nor any man known to history named [Martin Luther King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther_King_Jr. "Martin Luther King Jr.") ".[^7] Lelache's parents are [different races](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interracial_marriage "Interracial marriage"), so she never existed in that alternative reality. Orr dreams a gray version of her with a milder personality; the two marry.

After speaking with one of the aliens, who seem to have a cryptic understanding of effective dreams, Orr confronts Haber. In their final session, Haber "cures" Orr of his ability to dream effectively by suggesting Orr dream that his dreams no longer affect reality. Haber uses his Augmenter to give himself the power to dream effectively. Haber's first effective dream represents a significant break with the various realities created by Orr, and threatens to destroy reality. Despite Orr's efforts to prevent it, the gray Lelache is annihilated by the encroaching chaos. Orr successfully shuts off the Augmentor as coherent existence threatens to dissolve into undifferentiated chaos. The world is saved, but exists now as a mix of random elements from several realities.

In the new reality, Orr works at a kitchen store operated by one of the aliens. Haber survives, his mind shattered by his knowledge of unreality. Lelache is also restored, though she is left with only a slight memory of Orr. Orr is resigned to the loss of the Lelache he loved, but resolves to woo the version of Lelache that exists now. The story ends as the two have coffee, while Orr's inscrutable alien employer observes.

## Reception

[Theodore Sturgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Sturgeon "Theodore Sturgeon"), reviewing *Lathe* for *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")*, found it to be "a very good book", praising Le Guin for "produc\[ing\] a rare and powerful synthesis of poetry and science, reason and emotion".[^8] [Lester del Rey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_del_Rey "Lester del Rey"), however, faulted the novel for an arbitrary and ineffective second half, saying "with wonder piled on wonder, the plot simply loses credibility." [^9]

## Viewpoints

> One of the best novels, and most important to understanding of the nature of our world, is Ursula Le Guin's *The Lathe of Heaven*, in which the dream universe is articulated in such a striking and compelling way that I hesitate to add any further explanation to it; it requires none.

— Philip K. Dick

Although technology plays a minor role, the novel is concerned with philosophical questions about our desire to control our destiny, with Haber's [positivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positivism "Positivism") approach pitted against a [Taoist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoist "Taoist") equanimity. The beginnings of the chapters also feature quotes from [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells "H. G. Wells"), [Victor Hugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hugo "Victor Hugo") and [Taoist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoist "Taoist") sages. Due to its portrayal of psychologically derived [alternative realities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_universe_\(fiction\) "Parallel universe (fiction)"), the story is described as Le Guin's tribute to [Philip K. Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_K._Dick "Philip K. Dick").[^10] In his biography of Dick, Lawrence Sutin described Le Guin as having "long been a staunch public advocate of Phil's talent". According to Sutin, " *The Lathe of Heaven* was, by her acknowledgment, influenced by his \[Dick's\] sixties works." [^11]

The book is critical of [behaviorism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behaviorism "Behaviorism").[^12] Orr, a deceptively mild yet very strong and honest man, is labeled sick because he is immensely frightened by his ability to change reality. He is forced to undergo treatment. His efforts to get rid of Haber are viewed as suspect because he is a psychiatric patient. Haber, meanwhile, is very charming, [extraverted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extravert "Extravert"), and confident, yet he eventually goes insane and almost destroys reality. He dismisses Orr's qualms about meddling with reality with [paternalistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paternalistic "Paternalistic") [psychobabble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychobabble "Psychobabble"), and is more concerned with his machine and Orr's powers than with curing his patient.

The book is also critical of the philosophy of [utilitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism "Utilitarianism"), satirizing the phrase "The Greatest Happiness for the Greatest Number." It is critical of [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics "Eugenics"), which it suggests would be a key feature of a culture based on utilitarian ethics.

The book also the explores the negative consequences of the [will to power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_to_power "Will to power") in Haber's philosophy.[^13]

It has been suggested that Le Guin named her protagonist "George Orr" as an homage to British author [George Orwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Orwell "George Orwell"),[^14] as well as to draw comparisons between the dystopic worlds she describes in *Lathe* and the dystopia Orwell envisioned in his novel *[1984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nineteen_Eighty-Four "Nineteen Eighty-Four")*. It might also have the additional meaning *either / or*.[^15]

## Adaptations

An adaptation titled *[The Lathe of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lathe_of_Heaven_\(film\) "The Lathe of Heaven (film)")*, produced by the [public television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_broadcasting "Public broadcasting") station [WNET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WNET "WNET"), and directed by [David Loxton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Loxton "David Loxton") and [Fred Barzyk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Barzyk "Fred Barzyk"), was released in 1980. It was the first direct-to-TV film production by [Public Broadcasting Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PBS "PBS") (PBS) and was produced with a budget of $250,000. Generally faithful to the novel, it stars [Bruce Davison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruce_Davison "Bruce Davison") as George Orr, [Kevin Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kevin_Conway_\(actor\) "Kevin Conway (actor)") as William Haber, and [Margaret Avery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Avery "Margaret Avery") as Heather Lelache. Le Guin was heavily involved in the production of the 1980 adaptation, and expressed her satisfaction with it several times.[^5] [^16]

PBS's rights to rebroadcast the film expired in 1988, and it became the most-requested program in PBS history. Fans were extremely critical of [WNET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WNET "WNET") 's supposed "warehousing" of the film, but the budgetary barriers to rebroadcast were high: The station needed to pay for and clear rights with all participants in the original program; negotiate a special agreement with the composer of the film's score; and deal with [The Beatles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Beatles "The Beatles") recording excerpted in the original soundtrack, " [With a Little Help from My Friends](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/With_a_Little_Help_from_My_Friends "With a Little Help from My Friends") ", which is an integral plot point in both the novel and the film. A cover version replaces the Beatles' own recording in the home video release.

The home video release is remastered from a video tape of the original broadcast. PBS, anticipating that the rights issues would beset the production forever, did not save a copy of the film production in their archives.

A second adaptation was released in 2002 and retitled *[Lathe of Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lathe_of_Heaven_\(film\) "Lathe of Heaven (film)")*. Produced for the [A&E Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26E_\(TV_channel\) "A&E (TV channel)") and directed by [Philip Haas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Haas "Philip Haas"), the film starred [James Caan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Caan "James Caan"), [Lukas Haas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lukas_Haas "Lukas Haas"), and [Lisa Bonet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisa_Bonet "Lisa Bonet"). The 2002 adaptation discards a significant portion of the plot and some of the characters. Le Guin had no involvement in making the film.[^17]

A stage adaptation by [Edward Einhorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Einhorn "Edward Einhorn"), produced by Untitled Theater Company #61, ran from June 6 to June 30, 2012, at the 3LD Art + Technology Center in New York City.[^18]
