**Stanisław Herman Lem** (Polish: [\[staˈɲiswaf ˈlɛm\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/Polish "Help:IPA/Polish") ; 12 September 1921 – 27 March 2006) was a Polish writer. He was the author of many novels, short stories, and essays on various subjects, including philosophy, [futurology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurology "Futurology"), and [literary criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_criticism "Literary criticism"). Many of his science fiction stories are of [satirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satirical "Satirical") and humorous character. Lem's books have been translated into more than 50 languages and have sold more than 45 million copies.[^4] [^5] [^6] Worldwide, he is best known as the author of the 1961 novel *[Solaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_\(novel\) "Solaris (novel)")*. In 1976, [Theodore Sturgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Sturgeon "Theodore Sturgeon") wrote that Lem was the most widely read science fiction writer in the world.[^7]

Lem was the author of the fundamental philosophical work *[Summa Technologiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Technologiae "Summa Technologiae")*, in which he anticipated the creation of [virtual reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_reality "Virtual reality"), [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), and also developed the ideas of human autoevolution, the creation of [artificial worlds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulacrum "Simulacrum"), and many others. Lem's science fiction works explore philosophical themes through speculations on technology, the nature of [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence "Intelligence"), the impossibility of communication with and understanding of [alien intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alien_intelligence "Alien intelligence"), despair about human limitations, and humanity's place in the universe. His essays and philosophical books cover these and many other topics. Translating his works is difficult due to [Lem's elaborate neologisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neologisms_of_Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem "Neologisms of Stanisław Lem") and [idiomatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idiomatic "Idiomatic") wordplay.

The [Sejm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sejm "Sejm") (the lower house of the [Polish Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Parliament "Polish Parliament")) declared 2021 [Stanisław Lem Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem_Year "Stanisław Lem Year").[^8]

## Life

### Early life

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f6/4_Lepkoho_Street%2C_Lviv_%2802%29.jpg/250px-4_Lepkoho_Street%2C_Lviv_%2802%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)

House No. 4 on Bohdan Lepky Street in Lviv, where, according to his autobiography Highcastle, Lem spent his childhood

Lem was born in 1921 in Lwów, [interwar Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic "Second Polish Republic") (now [Lviv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv "Lviv"), Ukraine). According to his own account, he was actually born on 13 September, but the date was changed to the 12th on his birth certificate [because of superstition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskaidekaphobia "Triskaidekaphobia").[^9] He was the son of Sabina née Woller (1892–1979) and Samuel Lem [^1] (1879–1954), a wealthy [laryngologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laryngology "Laryngology") and former physician in the [Austro-Hungarian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Army "Austro-Hungarian Army"),[^11] [^12] and first cousin to Polish poet [Marian Hemar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_Hemar "Marian Hemar") (Lem's father's sister's son).[^13] In later years Lem sometimes claimed to have been raised [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic "Roman Catholic"), but he went to Jewish religious lessons during his school years.[^3] He later became an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheist "Atheist") "for moral reasons... the world appears to me to be put together in such a painful way that I prefer to believe that it was not created... intentionally".[^14] [^15] In later years he would call himself both an agnostic [^16] and an atheist.[^17]

After the 1939 [Soviet invasion of Poland's former eastern territory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_invasion_of_Poland "Soviet invasion of Poland") (now part of Ukraine and Belarus), he was not allowed to study at [Lwów Polytechnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lviv_Polytechnic#History "Lviv Polytechnic") as he wished because of his " [bourgeois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bourgeoisie "Bourgeoisie") origin"; it was due to his father's connections that he was accepted to study medicine at [Lwów University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Lviv "University of Lviv") in 1940.[^18] During the subsequent [Nazi occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupation_of_Poland_\(1939%E2%80%9345\) "Occupation of Poland (1939–45)") (1941–1944), Lem's Jewish family avoided placement in the Nazi [Lwów Ghetto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lw%C3%B3w_Ghetto "Lwów Ghetto"), surviving with false papers.[^12] He would later recall:[^12] [^19]

> During that period, I learned in a very personal, practical way that I was no " [Aryan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_race "Nordic race") ". I knew that my ancestors were Jews, but I knew nothing of the [Mosaic faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic_faith "Mosaic faith") and, regrettably, nothing at all of [Jewish culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_culture "Jewish culture"). So it was, strictly speaking, only the [Nazi legislation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Racial_policy_of_Nazi_Germany "Racial policy of Nazi Germany") that brought home to me the realization that I had Jewish blood in my veins.

During that time, Lem earned a living as a car mechanic and welder,[^12] and occasionally stole munitions from storehouses (to which he had access as an employee of a German company) to pass them on to the [Polish resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_resistance_movement_in_World_War_II "Polish resistance movement in World War II").[^20]

In 1945, Lwów was annexed into the [Soviet Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Ukraine "Soviet Ukraine"), and the family, along with many other Polish citizens, [was resettled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_exchange_between_Poland_and_Soviet_Ukraine "Population exchange between Poland and Soviet Ukraine") to [Kraków](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w "Kraków"), where Lem, at his father's insistence, took up medical studies at the [Jagiellonian University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagiellonian_University "Jagiellonian University").[^12] He did not take his final examinations on purpose, to avoid the career of military doctor, which he suspected could have become lifelong.[^21] [^18] [^2] After receiving *[absolutorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutorium "Absolutorium")* (Latin term for the evidence of completion of the studies without diploma), he did an obligatory monthly work at a hospital, at a maternity ward, where he assisted at a number of childbirths and a [caesarean section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesarean_section "Caesarean section"). Lem said that the sight of blood was one of the reasons he decided to drop medicine.[^22]

### Rise to fame

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a4/Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem.jpg/250px-Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)

Stanisław Lem and toy cosmonaut, 1966

Lem started his literary work in 1946 with a number of publications in different genres, including poetry, as well as his first science fiction novel, *[The Man from Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Man_from_Mars "The Man from Mars")*, serialized in *[Nowy Świat Przygód](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Nowy_%C5%9Awiat_Przyg%C3%B3d&action=edit&redlink=1 "Nowy Świat Przygód (page does not exist)")* (*New World of Adventures*).[^12] Between 1948 and 1950 Lem was working as a scientific research assistant at the [Jagiellonian University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagiellonian_University "Jagiellonian University"), and published a number of short stories, poems, reviews, etc., particularly in the magazine *[Tygodnik Powszechny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tygodnik_Powszechny "Tygodnik Powszechny")*.[^23] In 1951, he published his first book, *[The Astronauts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Astronauts "The Astronauts")*.[^12] [^24] In 1954, he published a short story collection, *[Sezam i inne opowiadania](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Sezam_i_inne_opowiadania&action=edit&redlink=1 "Sezam i inne opowiadania (page does not exist)")* \[*Sesame and Other Stories*\].[^12] The following year, 1955, saw the publication of another science fiction novel, *[The Magellanic Cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Magellanic_Cloud "The Magellanic Cloud")*.[^12]

During the era of [Stalinism in Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stalinism_in_Poland "Stalinism in Poland"), which had begun in the late 1940s, all published works had to be directly approved by the state.[^25] Thus *The Astronauts* was not, in fact, the first novel Lem finished, just the first that made it past the state censors.[^12] Going by the date of the finished manuscript, Lem's first book was a partly autobiographical novel *[Hospital of the Transfiguration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_of_the_Transfiguration "Hospital of the Transfiguration")*, finished in 1948.[^12] It would be published seven years later, in 1955, as a part of the trilogy *[Czas nieutracony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czas_nieutracony "Czas nieutracony")* (*Time Not Lost*).[^12] The experience of trying to push *Czas nieutracony* through the censors was one of the major reasons Lem decided to focus on the less-censored genre of science fiction.[^23] Nonetheless, most of Lem's works published in the 1950s also contain various elements of [socialist realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_realism "Socialist realism") as well as of the "glorious future of communism" forced upon him by the censors and editors.[^23] [^26] Lem later criticized several of his early pieces as compromised by the ideological pressure.[^12]

Lem became truly productive after 1956, when the [de-Stalinization period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khrushchev_Thaw "Khrushchev Thaw") in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union "Soviet Union") led to the " [Polish October](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_October "Polish October") ", when Poland experienced an increase in [freedom of speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freedom_of_speech "Freedom of speech").[^12] [^23] [^26] Between 1956 and 1968, Lem authored seventeen books.

In 1957, he published his first non-fiction, philosophical book, *[Dialogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogs_\(Lem\) "Dialogs (Lem)")*, as well as a science fiction anthology, *[The Star Diaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Star_Diaries "The Star Diaries")*,[^12] collecting short stories about one of his most popular characters, [Ijon Tichy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ijon_Tichy "Ijon Tichy").[^27] 1959 saw the publication of three books: the novels *[Eden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eden_\(Lem_novel\) "Eden (Lem novel)")* and *[The Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Investigation "The Investigation")*, and the short story anthology *An Invasion from Aldebaran* (*Inwazja z Aldebarana*).[^12] 1961 saw the novels *[Memoirs Found in a Bathtub](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoirs_Found_in_a_Bathtub "Memoirs Found in a Bathtub")*, *[Solaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_\(novel\) "Solaris (novel)")*, and *[Return from the Stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Return_from_the_Stars "Return from the Stars")*, with *Solaris* being among his top works.[^12] This was followed by a collection of his essays and non-fiction prose, *Wejście na orbitę* (1962), and a short story anthology *Noc księżycowa* (1963).[^12] In 1964, Lem published a large work on the border of philosophy and sociology of science and futurology, *[Summa Technologiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Technologiae "Summa Technologiae")*, as well as a novel, *[The Invincible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invincible "The Invincible")*.[^12] [^26]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/40/Stanislaw_Lem_by_Kubik.JPG/250px-Stanislaw_Lem_by_Kubik.JPG?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)

Lem signing in Kraków, 30 October 2005

1965 saw the publication of *[The Cyberiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cyberiad "The Cyberiad")* and of a short story collection, *The Hunt* ([Polowanie](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Polowanie&action=edit&redlink=1 "Polowanie (page does not exist)")).[^12] 1966 was the year of *[Highcastle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highcastle "Highcastle")*, followed in 1968 by *[His Master's Voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Master%27s_Voice_\(novel\) "His Master's Voice (novel)")* and *[Tales of Pirx the Pilot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tales_of_Pirx_the_Pilot "Tales of Pirx the Pilot")*.[^12] [^26] *Highcastle* was another of Lem's autobiographical works, and touched upon a theme that usually was not favored by the censors: Lem's youth in the pre-war, then-Polish, Lviv.[^12] 1968 and 1970 saw two more non-fiction treatises, *[The Philosophy of Chance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Philosophy_of_Chance "The Philosophy of Chance")* and *[Science Fiction and Futurology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Fiction_and_Futurology "Science Fiction and Futurology")*.[^12] Ijon Tichy returned in 1971's *[The Futurological Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Futurological_Congress "The Futurological Congress")*; in the same year Lem released a genre-mixing experiment, *[A Perfect Vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Perfect_Vacuum "A Perfect Vacuum")*, a [collection of reviews of non-existent books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem%27s_fictitious_criticism_of_nonexistent_books "Stanisław Lem's fictitious criticism of nonexistent books").[^12] In 1973 a similar work, *[Imaginary Magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_Magnitude "Imaginary Magnitude")*, was published.[^12] In 1976, Lem published two works: " [The Mask](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mask_\(Lem_short_story\) "The Mask (Lem short story)") " and *[The Chain of Chance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chain_of_Chance "The Chain of Chance")*.[^12] In 1980, he published another set of reviews of non-existent works, *[Provocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem%27s_fictitious_criticism_of_nonexistent_books#Provocation_and_One_Human_Minute "Stanisław Lem's fictitious criticism of nonexistent books")*.[^12] The following year saw another Tichy novel, *[Observation on the Spot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation_on_the_Spot "Observation on the Spot")*,[^12] and *[Golem XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golem_XIV "Golem XIV")*. Later in that decade, Lem published *[Peace on Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peace_on_Earth_\(novel\) "Peace on Earth (novel)")* (1984) and *[Fiasco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiasco_\(novel\) "Fiasco (novel)")* (1986), his last science fiction novel.[^12]

In the late 1970s and early 1980s, Lem cautiously supported the [Polish dissident movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_dissident_movement "Polish dissident movement"), and started publishing essays in the Paris-based magazine *[Kultura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kultura "Kultura")*.[^12] In 1982, with [martial law in Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_law_in_Poland "Martial law in Poland") declared, Lem moved to [West Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Berlin "West Berlin"), where he became a fellow of the [Institute for Advanced Study, Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Advanced_Study,_Berlin "Institute for Advanced Study, Berlin") (*Wissenschaftskolleg zu Berlin*).[^12] After that, he settled in [Vienna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vienna "Vienna"). He returned to Poland in 1988.[^12]

### Final years

From the late 1980s onwards, Lem tended to concentrate on philosophical texts and essays, published in Polish magazines including *Tygodnik Powszechny*, *[Odra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odra_\(magazine\) "Odra (magazine)")*, and *[Przegląd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Przegl%C4%85d "Przegląd")*.[^12] [^23] These were later collected in a number of anthologies.[^12]

In the early 1980s literary critic and historian [Stanisław Bereś](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Bere%C5%9B "Stanisław Bereś") conducted a lengthy interview with Lem, which was published in book format in 1987 as *[Rozmowy ze Stanisławem Lemem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rozmowy_ze_Stanis%C5%82awem_Lemem "Rozmowy ze Stanisławem Lemem")* (*Conversations with Stanisław Lem)*. That edition was subject to censorship. A revised, complete edition was published in 2002 as *Tako rzecze… Lem* (*Thus spoke... Lem*).[^28]

In the early 1990s, Lem met with the literary critic and scholar [Peter Swirski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Swirski "Peter Swirski") for a series of extensive interviews, published together with other critical materials and translations as *[A Stanislaw Lem Reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Stanislaw_Lem_Reader "A Stanislaw Lem Reader")* (1997). In these interviews Lem speaks about a range of issues he rarely discussed previously. The book also includes Swirski's translation of Lem's retrospective essay "Thirty Years Later", devoted to Lem's nonfictional treatise *Summa Technologiae*. During later interviews in 2005, Lem expressed his disappointment with the genre of science fiction, and his general pessimism regarding technical progress. He viewed the human body as unsuitable for space travel, held that information technology [drowns people in a glut of low-quality information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lem%27s_Law "Lem's Law"), and considered truly intelligent robots as both undesirable and impossible to construct.[^29]

## Writings

### Science fiction

Lem's prose shows a mastery of numerous genres and themes.[^12]

#### Recurring themes

One of Lem's major recurring themes, beginning from his very first novel, *[The Man from Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Man_from_Mars "The Man from Mars")*, was the impossibility of [communication between profoundly alien beings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communication_with_extraterrestrial_intelligence "Communication with extraterrestrial intelligence"), which may have no common ground with human intelligence, and humans.[^30] The best known example is the living planetary ocean in *[Solaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_\(novel\) "Solaris (novel)")*. Other examples include the intelligent swarms of mechanical insect-like [micromachines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micromachines "Micromachines") in *[The Invincible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invincible "The Invincible")*, and strangely ordered societies of more human-like beings in *[Fiasco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiasco_\(novel\) "Fiasco (novel)")* and *[Eden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eden_\(Lem_novel\) "Eden (Lem novel)")*, describing the failure of [first contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_contact_\(science_fiction\) "First contact (science fiction)").

Another key recurring theme is the shortcomings of humans. In *[His Master's Voice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/His_Master%27s_Voice_\(novel\) "His Master's Voice (novel)")*, Lem describes the failure of humanity's intelligence to decipher and truly comprehend an apparent message from space.[^31] [^32] [^33] [^34] Two overlapping arcs of short stories, *[Fables for Robots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fables_for_Robots "Fables for Robots")* and *[The Cyberiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cyberiad "The Cyberiad")* provide a commentary on humanity in the form of a series of grotesque, humorous, [fairytale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairytale "Fairytale") -like short stories about a mechanical universe inhabited by robots (who have occasional contact with biological "slimies" and human "palefaces").[^12] [^35] Lem also underlines the uncertainties of evolution, including that it might not progress upwards in intelligence.[^36]

### Other writings

*[The Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Investigation "The Investigation")* and *[The Chain of Chance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chain_of_Chance "The Chain of Chance")* are [crime novels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_novel "Crime novel") (the latter without a murderer); *Pamiętnik...* is a psychological drama inspired by [Kafka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Kafka "Franz Kafka").[^12] *A Perfect Vacuum* and *Imaginary Magnitude* are [collections of reviews of and introductions to non-existent books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem%27s_fictitious_criticism_of_nonexistent_books "Stanisław Lem's fictitious criticism of nonexistent books").[^12] Similarly, *Provocations* purports to review a non-existent [Holocaust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holocaust "Holocaust") -themed work.[^12]

### Essays

*[Dialogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogs_\(Lem\) "Dialogs (Lem)")* and *[Summa Technologiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Technologiae "Summa Technologiae")* (1964) are Lem's two most famous philosophical texts. The *Summa* is notable for being a unique analysis of prospective social, cybernetic, and biological advances;[^12] in this work, Lem discusses philosophical implications of technologies that were completely in the realm of science fiction at the time, but are gaining importance today—for instance, [virtual reality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_reality "Virtual reality") and [nanotechnology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanotechnology "Nanotechnology").

### Views in later life

Throughout the entirety of his life, Stanisław Lem remained deeply attached to his original hometown of Lwów (then in Poland, now Lviv in Ukraine) and missed it greatly. Although he never called for Poland to retake the city, he expressed sorrow and felt a sense of injustice at Poland losing the city to the USSR after the Second World War.[^37] [^38] [^39] His criticism of most science fiction surfaced in literary and philosophical essays *Science Fiction and Futurology* and interviews.[^40] In the 1990s, Lem forswore science fiction [^41] and returned to futurological prognostications, most notably those expressed in *[Okamgnienie](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Okamgnienie&action=edit&redlink=1 "Okamgnienie (page does not exist)")* \[*Blink of an Eye*\]. He had a deep appreciation for the works of Polish writer [Czesław Miłosz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czes%C5%82aw_Mi%C5%82osz "Czesław Miłosz") and respected [Józef Piłsudski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J%C3%B3zef_Pi%C5%82sudski "Józef Piłsudski") as a national leader.[^37]

Lem said that since the success of the trade union [Solidarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solidarity_\(Polish_trade_union\) "Solidarity (Polish trade union)"), and the [collapse of the Soviet empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collapse_of_Communism "Collapse of Communism"), he felt his wild dreams about the future could no longer compare with reality.[^42] He became increasingly critical of modern technology in his later life, criticising inventions such as the Internet, which he said "makes it easier to hurt our neighbors." [^43] He was a proponent of [nuclear power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power "Nuclear power"), which he saw as a potential means for Poland to secure its sovereignty via reducing dependency on fossil fuels from Russia.[^37] In his 2004-2006 columns for *[Tygodnik Powszechny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tygodnik_Powszechny "Tygodnik Powszechny")*, Lem was highly critical of [Vladimir Putin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Putin "Vladimir Putin"), [George W. Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Bush "George W. Bush"), [Andrzej Lepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrzej_Lepper "Andrzej Lepper"), [Samoobrona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Defence_of_the_Republic_of_Poland "Self-Defence of the Republic of Poland"), the [League of Polish Families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/League_of_Polish_Families "League of Polish Families"), and the [All-Polish Youth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All-Polish_Youth "All-Polish Youth").[^37]

## Relationship with American science fiction

### SFWA

Lem was awarded an honorary membership in the [Science Fiction Writers of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Fiction_and_Fantasy_Writers_of_America "Science Fiction and Fantasy Writers of America") (SFWA) in 1973. SFWA honorary membership is given to people who do not meet the publishing criteria for joining the regular membership, but who would be welcomed as members had their work appeared in the qualifying English-language publications. Lem never had a high opinion of American [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction "Science fiction"), describing it as ill-thought-out, poorly written, and interested more in making money than in ideas or new literary forms.[^44] After his eventual American publication, when he became eligible for regular membership, his honorary membership was rescinded. This formal action was interpreted by some SFWA members as a rebuke for his stance,[^45] and it seems that Lem interpreted it as such. Lem was invited to stay on with the organization with a regular membership, but he declined.[^46] After many members (including [Ursula K. Le Guin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursula_K._Le_Guin "Ursula K. Le Guin"), who quit her membership and then refused the [Nebula Award for Best Novelette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula_Award_for_Best_Novelette "Nebula Award for Best Novelette") for *[The Diary of the Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Diary_of_the_Rose "The Diary of the Rose")*) [^47] [^48] protested against Lem's treatment by the SFWA, a member offered to pay his dues. Lem never accepted the offer.[^44] [^46]

### Philip K. Dick

Lem singled out only one [^49] American science fiction writer for praise, [Philip K. Dick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_K._Dick "Philip K. Dick"), in a 1984 English-language anthology of his critical essays, *[Microworlds: Writings on Science Fiction and Fantasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microworlds:_Writings_on_Science_Fiction_and_Fantasy "Microworlds: Writings on Science Fiction and Fantasy")*. Lem had initially held a low opinion of Philip K. Dick (as he did for the bulk of American science fiction) and would later say that this was due to a limited familiarity with Dick's work, since Western literature was hard to come by in the [Polish People's Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_People%27s_Republic "Polish People's Republic").

Dick alleged that Stanisław Lem was probably a false name used by a composite committee operating on orders of the [Communist party](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communist_party "Communist party") to gain control over public opinion, and wrote a letter to the [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FBI "FBI") to that effect.[^50] There were several attempts to explain Dick's act. Lem was responsible for the Polish translation of Dick's work *[Ubik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ubik "Ubik")* in 1972, and when Dick felt monetarily short-changed by the publisher, he held Lem personally responsible (see *Microworlds*).[^51] [^50] Also it was suggested that Dick was under the influence of strong medications, including opioids, and may have experienced a "slight disconnect from reality" some time before writing the letter.[^50] A "defensive patriotism" of Dick against [Lem's attacks on American science fiction](#SFWA) may have played some role as well.[^50] Lem would later mention Dick in his monograph *Science Fiction and Futurology*.

## Significance

### Writing

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f3/Lem-first_polish_editions_collection2.jpg/500px-Lem-first_polish_editions_collection2.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)

First Polish editions of books by Lem

Lem is one of the most highly acclaimed science fiction writers, hailed by critics as equal to such classic authors as [H. G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._G._Wells "H. G. Wells") and [Olaf Stapledon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olaf_Stapledon "Olaf Stapledon").[^52] In 1976, [Theodore Sturgeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Sturgeon "Theodore Sturgeon") wrote that Lem was the most widely read science fiction writer in the world.[^7] In Poland, in the 1960s and 1970s, Lem remained under the radar of mainstream critics, who dismissed him as a "mass market", low-brow, youth-oriented writer; such dismissal might have given him a form of invisibility from censorship.[^12]

In 1999, [Franz Rottensteiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Rottensteiner "Franz Rottensteiner"), Lem's former agent abroad, had this to say about Lem's reception on international markets:[^53]

> With \[number of translations and copies sold\], Lem is the most successful author in modern Polish fiction; nevertheless his commercial success in the world is limited, and the bulk of his large editions was due to the special publishing conditions in the [Communist countries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc "Eastern Bloc"): Poland, the Soviet Union, and the [German Democratic Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Democratic_Republic "German Democratic Republic"). Only in [West Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Germany "West Germany") was Lem really a critical and a commercial success \[... and everywhere...\] in recent years interest in him has waned. Lem is the only writer of European \[science fiction, most of whose\] books have been translated into English, and \[...\] kept in print in the USA. Lem's critical success in English is due mostly to the excellent translations of [Michael Kandel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Kandel "Michael Kandel").

Lem's works were widely translated abroad, appearing in more than 40 languages [^12] and have sold more than 45 million copies.[^4] [^5] [^6] As of 2020, about 1.5 million copies were sold in Poland after his death, with the annual numbers of 100,000 matching the new bestsellers.[^54]

### Influence

[Will Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Wright_\(game_designer\) "Will Wright (game designer)") 's popular city-planning game *[SimCity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SimCity_\(1989_video_game\) "SimCity (1989 video game)")* was partly inspired by Lem's short story " [The Seventh Sally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Seventh_Sally "The Seventh Sally") " in *The Cyberiad*.[^55]

The video game *[Stellaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellaris_\(video_game\) "Stellaris (video game)")* is highly inspired by his works, as its creators said at the start of 2021,[^56] designated the " [Year of Lem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Year_of_Lem "Year of Lem") ".

A major character in the film *[Planet 51](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet_51 "Planet 51")*, an alien Lem, was named by screenwriter [Joe Stillman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joe_Stillman "Joe Stillman") after Stanisław Lem. Since the film was intended to be a parody of American [pulp science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulp_magazine "Pulp magazine") shot in Eastern Europe, Stillman thought that it would be hilarious to hint at the writer whose works have nothing to do with [little green men](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_green_men "Little green men").[^57]

## Adaptations of Lem's works

*Solaris* was made into [a film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_\(1968_film\) "Solaris (1968 film)") in 1968 by Russian director Boris Nirenburg, [a film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_\(1972_film\) "Solaris (1972 film)") in 1972 by Russian director [Andrei Tarkovsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Tarkovsky "Andrei Tarkovsky") —which won a Special Jury Prize at the [Cannes Film Festival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannes_Film_Festival "Cannes Film Festival") in 1972—and [an American film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solaris_\(2002_film\) "Solaris (2002 film)") in 2002 by [Steven Soderbergh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Soderbergh "Steven Soderbergh"). Film critics have noted the influence of Tarkovsky's adaptation on later science fiction films such as [*Event Horizon*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Event_Horizon_\(film\) "Event Horizon (film)") (1997) [^58] [^59] and [Christopher Nolan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Nolan "Christopher Nolan") 's *[Inception](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inception "Inception")* (2010).[^60] [^61]

[A number of other dramatic and musical adaptations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_works_by_Stanis%C5%82aw_Lem_and_their_adaptations#Adaptations "List of works by Stanisław Lem and their adaptations") of his work exist, such as adaptations of *The Astronauts* (*[First Spaceship on Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Spaceship_on_Venus "First Spaceship on Venus")*, 1960) and *The Magellanic Cloud* (*[Ikarie XB-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikarie_XB-1 "Ikarie XB-1")*, 1963).[^62] Lem himself was, however, critical of most of the screen adaptations, with the sole exception of *[Przekładaniec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Przek%C5%82adaniec "Przekładaniec")* in 1968 by [Andrzej Wajda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrzej_Wajda "Andrzej Wajda").[^12] In 2013, the Israeli–Polish co-production *[The Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Congress_\(2013_film\) "The Congress (2013 film)")* was released, inspired by Lem's novel *The Futurological Congress*.[^63]

[György Pálfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gy%C3%B6rgy_P%C3%A1lfi "György Pálfi") directed a film adaptation of *His Master's Voice* with the same title, which was released in 2018.

In 2023, [11 Bit Studios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/11_Bit_Studios "11 Bit Studios") published *[The Invincible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invincible_\(video_game\) "The Invincible (video game)")*, an adventure video game developed by Starward Industries. The game is an adaptation of Stanisław Lem's [1964 novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Invincible "The Invincible").

## Political views

Lem's early works were [socialist realist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialist_realist "Socialist realist"), possibly to satisfy state censorship.[^76] In his later years, Lem was critical of this aspect of his work.[^77] In 1982, with the onset of the [martial law in Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martial_law_in_Poland "Martial law in Poland"), Lem moved to Berlin for studies; between 1983 and 1988, he lived in Vienna.[^78] He never showed any wish to relocate permanently in the West. By the standards of the [Eastern Bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Bloc "Eastern Bloc"), Lem was financially well off for most of his life.[^79] Lem was a critic of [capitalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capitalism "Capitalism"),[^80] [totalitarianism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totalitarianism "Totalitarianism"), and of both Stalinist and Western ideologies.[^81]

Lem believed there were no absolutes. He said: "I should wish, as do most men, that immutable truths existed, that not all would be eroded by the impact of historical time, that there were some essential propositions, be it only in the field of human values, the basic values, etc. In brief, I long for the absolute. But at the same time I am firmly convinced that there are no absolutes, that everything is historical, and that you cannot get away from history." [^82] Lem was concerned that if the human race attained prosperity and comfort, this would lead it to passiveness and degeneration.[^77]

## Personal life

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f0/Gr%C3%B3b_Stanis%C5%82awa_Lema.jpg/250px-Gr%C3%B3b_Stanis%C5%82awa_Lema.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)

Stanisław Lem's grave at the Salwator Cemetery, Kraków

Lem was a [polyglot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyglot "Polyglot"): he knew Polish, Latin (from medical school), German, French, English, Russian and Ukrainian.[^83] Lem claimed that his [IQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IQ "IQ") was tested at high school as 180.[^84]

In 1953, Lem met [radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiology "Radiology") student Barbara Leśniak, whom he married in a [civil ceremony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_marriage "Civil marriage") the same year.[^85] [^86] The couple's [church marriage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marriage_in_the_Catholic_Church "Marriage in the Catholic Church") ceremony was performed in February 1954.[^12] Barbara died on 27 April 2016.[^87] Their only child, [Tomasz](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Tomasz_Lem&action=edit&redlink=1 "Tomasz Lem (page does not exist)") (born 1968), who graduated with a degree in physics from [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University "Princeton University"), has written *Awantury na tle powszechnego ciążenia* (*Tantrums on the Background of the Universal Gravitation*), a memoir which contains numerous personal details about Lem. The book jacket says Tomasz works as a translator and has a daughter, Anna.[^88]

As of 1984, Lem's writing pattern was to get up a short time before five in the morning and start writing soon after; Lem would write for five or six hours before taking a break.[^89]

Lem was an aggressive driver. He loved sweets (especially [halva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halva "Halva") and chocolate-covered [marzipan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marzipan "Marzipan")), and did not give them up even when, toward the end of his life, he fell ill with [diabetes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diabetes "Diabetes"). Due to health problems, Lem stopped smoking in the mid-1980s.[^77] Coffee often featured in Lem's writing and interviews.[^90] [^91] [^92] [^93] [^94]

Stanisław Lem died from a heart failure [^95] in the hospital of the [Jagiellonian University Medical College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jagiellonian_University_Medical_College "Jagiellonian University Medical College"), [Kraków](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krak%C3%B3w "Kraków") on 27 March 2006 at the age of 84.[^23] He was buried at [Salwator Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salwator_Cemetery "Salwator Cemetery"), Sector W, Row 4, grave 17 ([Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language "Polish language"): *cmentarz Salwatorski, sektor W, rząd 4, grób 17*).[^96]

In November 2021, [Agnieszka Gajewska](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Agnieszka_Gajewska&action=edit&redlink=1 "Agnieszka Gajewska (page does not exist)") 's biography of Lem, *Holocaust and the Stars*, was translated into English by [Katarzyna Gucio](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Katarzyna_Gucio&action=edit&redlink=1 "Katarzyna Gucio (page does not exist)") and published by [Routledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routledge "Routledge").[^97] [^98] It discussed aspects of Lem's life, such as being forced to wear the [yellow badge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yellow_badge "Yellow badge") and being struck for not removing his hat in the presence of Germans, as required of Jews at the time.

Lem loved movies and greatly enjoyed [artistic cinema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_film "Art film") (especially the movies of [Luis Buñuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luis_Bu%C3%B1uel "Luis Buñuel")). He also liked [King Kong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Kong "King Kong"), [James Bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Bond "James Bond"), *[Star Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars "Star Wars")*, and *[Star Trek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek "Star Trek")* [^99] movies but he remained mostly displeased by movies which were based upon his own stories.[^100] The only notable exceptions are *[Voyage to the End of the Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voyage_to_the_End_of_the_Universe "Voyage to the End of the Universe")* (1963) (which didn't credit Lem as writer of the original book *The Magellanic Cloud*) and *[Przekładaniec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Przek%C5%82adaniec "Przekładaniec")* (*Layer Cake*) (1968) (which was based upon his short story "Do You Exist, Mr Jones?").[^101]
