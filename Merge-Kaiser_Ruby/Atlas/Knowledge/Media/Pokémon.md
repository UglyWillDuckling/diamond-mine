***Pokémon*** [^2] [^3] is a Japanese [media franchise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_franchise "Media franchise") consisting of [video games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Pok%C3%A9mon_video_games "List of Pokémon video games"), [animated series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_\(TV_series\) "Pokémon (TV series)") and [films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Pok%C3%A9mon_films "List of Pokémon films"), a [trading card game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Trading_Card_Game "Pokémon Trading Card Game"), and other related media. The franchise takes place in a [shared universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_universe "Shared universe") in which humans co-exist with the [eponymous creatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Pok%C3%A9mon "List of Pokémon"), a large variety of species which have special powers. The franchise's primary [target audience](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Target_audience "Target audience") is children aged 5 to 12,[^16] but it is known to attract people of all ages.[^478] *Pokémon* is estimated to be the world's [highest-grossing media franchise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_highest-grossing_media_franchises "List of highest-grossing media franchises"), with the video games in particular being among the [best-selling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_best-selling_video_game_franchises "List of best-selling video game franchises").

The franchise originated as [a pair of role-playing games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Red,_Blue,_and_Yellow "Pokémon Red, Blue, and Yellow") developed by [Game Freak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Freak "Game Freak"), from an original concept by its founder, [Satoshi Tajiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satoshi_Tajiri "Satoshi Tajiri"). Released on the [Game Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy "Game Boy") on 27 February 1996, the games became [sleeper hits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sleeper_hit "Sleeper hit") and were followed by manga series, a trading card game, and anime series and films. From 1998 to 2000, *Pokémon* was exported to the rest of the world, creating an unprecedented global phenomenon dubbed "Pokémania". By 2002, the craze had ended, after which *Pokémon* became a fixture in popular culture, with new products releasing to this day. In 2016, the franchise spawned a second craze with the release of the [AR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_reality "Augmented reality") game *[Pokémon Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Go "Pokémon Go")*, developed by [Niantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niantic,_Inc. "Niantic, Inc.").

The franchise is managed by [the Pokémon Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pok%C3%A9mon_Company "The Pokémon Company") (TPC), which was established in 2000 [^4] by the joint owners of the franchise: original video game series publisher [Nintendo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo "Nintendo"), Trading Card Game developer [Creatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creatures_\(company\) "Creatures (company)"), and Game Freak.[^13] The three companies also own the anime series alongside [TV Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TV_Tokyo "TV Tokyo"), [ShoPro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shogakukan-Shueisha_Productions "Shogakukan-Shueisha Productions"), and [JR Kikaku](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Japan_Railway_Company "East Japan Railway Company"),[^14] and Pikachu Project, a joint venture between The Pokémon Company, TV Tokyo, and ShoPro, owns the anime films.[^15] Since 2009, The Pokémon Company International (TPCi), a subsidiary of TPC, has managed the franchise in all regions outside Asia.[^26] [^27]

## Name

The original full name of the franchise is ***Pocket Monsters*** (ポケットモンスター, **Poketto Monsutā**), which has been [commonly abbreviated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_abbreviated_and_contracted_words "Japanese abbreviated and contracted words") to *Pokemon* (ポケモン) since its launch. When the franchise was released internationally, the short form of the title was used, with an [acute accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_accent "Acute accent") (´) over the *e* to aid in pronunciation.[^28]

*Pokémon* refers to both the franchise itself and the eponymous creatures. As a noun, it is [identical in both the singular and plural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_plurals#Nouns_with_identical_singular_and_plural "English plurals"), as is every individual species name.[^29] Thus, it is grammatically correct to say "one Pokémon" and "many Pokémon", as well as "one [Pikachu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pikachu "Pikachu") " and "many Pikachu".[^30]

## General concept

The *Pokémon* franchise takes place in a [shared universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shared_universe "Shared universe") in which humans co-exist with creatures known as Pokémon. [The original pair of games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Red_and_Green "Pokémon Red and Green") feature 151 Pokémon species, with new ones being added in subsequent games; as of early 2026, [1,025 Pokémon species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Pok%C3%A9mon "List of Pokémon") have been introduced.[^5] Most Pokémon are based on real-life animals or mythical creatures from folklore.[^31] For example, [Pikachu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pikachu "Pikachu") are a yellow, mouse-like species with tails shaped as lightning bolts, able to blast powerful electric charges through the air.[^32] [^33] [^34]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/b/b1/Pok%C3%A9ball.png/120px-Pok%C3%A9ball.png)

Poké Balls are used to capture Pokémon.

The [player character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Player_character "Player character") takes the role of a Pokémon Trainer. The Trainer has three primary aims: travel and explore the Pokémon world; discover and catch a specimen of each Pokémon species in order to complete their [Pokédex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9dex "Pokédex"); and train a team of Pokémon and have them engage in battles.[^35] [^36] [^37] Most Pokémon can be caught with spherical devices known as [Poké Balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9_Ball "Poké Ball"). Once the opposing Pokémon is sufficiently weakened, the Trainer throws the Poké Ball against the Pokémon, which is then transformed into a form of energy and transported into the device. If the catch is successful, the Pokémon is tamed and under the Trainer's command from then on. If the Poké Ball is thrown again, the Pokémon re-materializes into its original state.[^38] [^39] The Trainer's Pokémon can engage in battles against opposing Pokémon, including those in the wild or owned by other Trainers.[^40] Because the franchise is aimed at children, these battles are never presented as overtly violent and contain no blood or gore.[^479] Pokémon never die in battle – they [faint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syncope_\(medicine\) "Syncope (medicine)") upon being defeated, and can then be rejuvenated at a Pokémon Center.[^480]

## History

### Origins

The main idea behind *Pokémon* was conceived by [Satoshi Tajiri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satoshi_Tajiri "Satoshi Tajiri"). Tajiri was born on 28 August 1965,[^49] and grew up in [Machida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machida,_Tokyo "Machida, Tokyo"), a suburb of Tokyo.[^50] As a child, he enjoyed discovering and catching insects and other small creatures in the various ponds and fields that surrounded his town.[^51] [^52] During [Japan's economic miracle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_economic_miracle "Japanese economic miracle"), many cities, including Machida, were significantly expanded. [As a consequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_impact_on_the_environment "Human impact on the environment"), Machida's nature was largely destroyed. In his second year of [junior high school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_education_in_Japan#Junior_high_school "Secondary education in Japan"),[^53] [^54] an [arcade hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amusement_arcade "Amusement arcade") opened in Tajiri's neighborhood, introducing him to video games. While studying electrical engineering at [Tokyo College of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyo_College_of_Technology?action=edit&redlink=1 "Tokyo College of Technology (page does not exist)"), Tajiri began publishing a *[doujinshi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doujinshi "Doujinshi")* magazine titled *Game Freak*. The title was inspired by the 1932 film *[Freaks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freaks_\(1932_film\) "Freaks (1932 film)")*, which Tajiri was fascinated with at the time.[^55] He self-published the first issue of the magazine in March 1983, at the age of 17.[^56] [^57] At the time, magazines specializing in video games did not yet exist in Japan, allowing *Game Freak* to fill a [gap in the market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gap_in_the_market "Gap in the market").[^58] [^59] Sometime later, Tajiri was contacted by aspiring [manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga "Manga") artist [Ken Sugimori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Sugimori "Ken Sugimori"), who became *Game Freak'* s illustrator.[^60] *Game Freak* folded in the late 1980s,[^56] by which point Tajiri had become a respected [game journalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_journalism "Video game journalism") in Japan's fledgling video game industry.[^61] Through his work, Tajiri befriended [Tsunekazu Ishihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsunekazu_Ishihara "Tsunekazu Ishihara"), a Japanese business man who, among other things, produced game-related shows for [Fuji Television](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuji_Television "Fuji Television").[^62] [^63] [^64]

In 1986, Tajiri, Sugimori, and a few other enthusiasts started an informal development team called Game Freak, named after the magazine it grew out of. Over the next few years, they [independently developed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indie_game "Indie game") the puzzle game *[Quinty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendel_Palace "Mendel Palace")*, working on it alongside school or their regular jobs.[^65] However, no one within the group knew how to make the [game's music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_music "Video game music"). After consulting all his contacts, Tajiri got in touch with [Junichi Masuda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junichi_Masuda "Junichi Masuda"), who became the group's composer.[^66] *Quinty* was finished in 1989, and published by [Namco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Namco "Namco").[^67] Tajiri officially incorporated [Game Freak Co., Ltd.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Freak "Game Freak") on 26 April 1989.[^68]

### 1989–1995: Development of Red and Green

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Duo_gameboy.JPG/250px-Duo_gameboy.JPG)

Two original Game Boys connected with a Game Link Cable

Tajiri started to think of what was to become *Pokémon* while completing *Quinty*, and before he officially founded Game Freak. Around this time, [Nintendo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo "Nintendo") announced the upcoming release of the [Game Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy "Game Boy"), a handheld console that would revolutionize the gaming industry. Tajiri learned that the device would have a [link port](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_port_\(hardware\) "Computer port (hardware)"), and with the corresponding [Game Link Cable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Link_Cable "Game Link Cable"), two Game Boys could be linked together.[^69] Sometime later, Tajiri remembered an incident while playing *[Dragon Quest II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_Quest_II "Dragon Quest II")* (1987), a [role-playing game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-playing_game "Role-playing game") (RPG) for the Famicom ([NES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NES "NES")). The game features randomly appearing items of varying rarity, including an extremely rare item called Mysterious Hat.[^6] Tajiri did not encounter any, while Ken Sugimori, who was also playing the game, encountered two. Upon recalling this experience, Tajiri realized that the cable now made it possible to transfer things from one cartridge to another.[^481] He noted that, until then, the Game Link Cable was only used for competing, but not for something else.[^74] Combining this inspiration with his memories of catching insects and other small species, Tajiri's idea would eventually evolve into a virtual recreation of his boyhood experiences,[^75] and an attempt to "regain the world that he had lost".[^76] He would later state that the game represents "the story of a boy's summer day".[^77]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ce/Gachapon.jpg/250px-Gachapon.jpg)

Gashapon capsules have been cited as an inspiration for Pokemon.

Tajiri and his Game Freak staff began pondering over a game centered on capturing creatures of differing rarity. Since the Game Boy is a portable device, these creatures could then be exchanged with other players in real life using the link cable. Once the player has caught a creature in-game, it was to be stored in miniaturized form in a special capsule. This facet of the game was inspired by *[Ultraseven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraseven "Ultraseven")*, a *[tokusatsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokusatsu "Tokusatsu")* show that Tajiri had enjoyed as a child.[^78] [The series' titular character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraseven_\(character\) "Ultraseven (character)") owns a number of capsules containing miniaturized *[kaiju](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaiju "Kaiju")* (monsters), which come out and return to their original sizes when the capsule is thrown into mid-air. *Kaiju* media in general were an important influence on *Pokemon*, as many Game Freak staff members had grown up with them.[^71] [^79] Other influences that have been cited by Tajiri include: *[gashapon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gashapon "Gashapon")*, capsules with toy figures in them that can be drawn from vending machines;[^80] [^81] [collectible cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectible_card "Collectible card"), such as [baseball cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball_card "Baseball card"), *[Ultraman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraman "Ultraman")* cards and *[menko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menko "Menko")*;[^482] *[The Final Fantasy Legend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Final_Fantasy_Legend "The Final Fantasy Legend")* (1989), the first RPG for the Game Boy;[^86] and [petting in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pet_culture_in_Japan "Pet culture in Japan"), with Tajiri noting that having Pokemon is similar to having pets.[^87] Tajiri initially named his project *Capsule Monsters*, which GF's staff [commonly shortened](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_abbreviated_and_contracted_words "Japanese abbreviated and contracted words") to *Capumon*. However, it later turned out that the term *Capsule Monsters* could not be trademarked, and it was subsequently decided to call the game *Pocket Monsters*, which became *Pokemon*.[^88] [^89]

In March 1989, Nintendo and [Shigesato Itoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigesato_Itoi "Shigesato Itoi") co-founded Ape Inc., a company meant to give outside talent a chance to pitch new, innovative games.[^63] [^64] At the time, Ape was housed in the same [Kanda-Sudachō](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanda-Sudach%C5%8D "Kanda-Sudachō") office building as Nintendo, located in Tokyo.[^90] Ishihara, a friend of both Itoi and Tajiri, was involved with Ape's management (and would become its vice-president in 1991).[^91] [^63] Tajiri's relationship with Ishihara prompted Tajiri to present his idea for *Pocket Monsters* at Ape's office.[^92] [^93] Present during Tajiri's pitch was Takashi Kawaguchi, who worked at Nintendo's General Affairs Department and was also a manager at Ape.[^64] [^94] Kawaguchi brought the idea to Nintendo president [Hiroshi Yamauchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshi_Yamauchi "Hiroshi Yamauchi"), who reportedly said: "This is it. This is the idea I've been waiting for." [^95] The development contract was signed at the beginning of 1990, with a planned delivery of the game in October.[^96] Tajiri directed the project, working under Ishihara.[^97] Ishihara was the producer – he managed the budget, staff, and work schedule, monitored the game's overall progress, and served as a liaison between Game Freak and Nintendo.[^98] [^99] Ishihara also contributed ideas to the development,[^100] and helped with [debugging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debugging "Debugging").[^101] [^102] Sugimori was in charge of the graphics and character design.[^103] Masuda created all music and sound effects, and did part of the programming.[^104] The budget that Nintendo granted to Game Freak was low;[^96] thus, *Pocket Monsters* was initially planned as a small, compact game, based primarily around Tajiri's core idea of exchanging.[^86] [^105] However, as development progressed, GF's ideas and ambitions for *Pokemon* grew.[^86] They soon realized that the game they were beginning to envision would not be easy to make.[^106] *Pocket Monsters* was suspended indefinitely, and GF turned their focus on other titles (see [Game Freak § Games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Freak#Games "Game Freak")).

After the game's initial development phase in 1990 and 1991,[^107] the staff "tinkered with it from time to time", as Sugimori put it.[^108] Still, development had mostly come to a halt until the summer of 1994, after the release of *[Pulseman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulseman "Pulseman")*, upon which Tajiri decided it was time to make a serious effort towards finishing *Pocket Monsters*.[^109] By this point, Game Freak's experience had grown considerably. Over the years, a number of new staff members had been added to the company. One of them was [Atsuko Nishida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atsuko_Nishida "Atsuko Nishida"), a graphic artist who created [Pikachu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pikachu "Pikachu"), among others.[^110] [^111] [^112] Ishihara used his knowledge of card games to add more depth to the battle system, and among other things suggested Pokemon types. Ishihara also came up with the idea of the Pokedex, a portable encyclopedic device which players can use to keep track of the Pokemon they caught.[^100] On the suggestion of [Shigeru Miyamoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Miyamoto "Shigeru Miyamoto"), it was decided to release two editions of the game: a *Red* version and a *Green* version. Otherwise identical, each had Pokemon not found in the other, encouraging players to socialize and trade to complete their collection.[^113] [^114]

Ishihara aspired to create video games of his own.[^115] As *Pocket Monsters Red* and *Green* were nearing completion, Ishihara founded [Creatures, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creatures,_Inc. "Creatures, Inc.") on 8 November 1995.[^116] Co-ownership of the *Pokemon* property, which Ishihara helped create, was subsequently assigned to Creatures. This resulted in *Pokemon* having three legal owners: Game Freak, the main developer; Creatures, representing producer Ishihara; and Nintendo, the publisher. [Anne Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Allison "Anne Allison") wrote that Nintendo also bought the property after *Red* and *Green* were finished.[^117] Journalist Kenji Hatakeyama noted that the ownership structure of *Pokemon* is uncommon. He wrote that "Pokemon is probably the only property in the world today for which the original rights are not concentrated in a single company", like [The Walt Disney Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Walt_Disney_Company "The Walt Disney Company") does with their [IPs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_property "Intellectual property").[^118] Tajiri and Ishihara did consider merging Game Freak and Creatures at one point. However, Tajiri decided against it because he feared it would erase what he had built up since he was a teenager. "I felt threatened by the idea of changing how Game Freak was operating, and starting back over with Mr. Ishihara", he said. "It was an identity problem. If Game Freak ceased to exist, then so would I". Tajiri noted that, since Game Freak and Creatures both focus on *Pokemon*, it sometimes felt more like different departments than different companies.[^92]

### 1996–1998: Rise in Japan

#### CoroCoro manga, Mew lottery

After finally being finished in December 1995,[^119] [*Pocket Monsters Red* and *Green*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Red_and_Blue "Pokémon Red and Blue") were released on 27 February 1996. Nintendo had no high expectations of the games, and media largely ignored them.[^120] [By 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1996_in_video_games "1996 in video games"), the seven-year-old [Game Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy "Game Boy") console was considered dated and near the [end of its lifecycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/End-of-life_product "End-of-life product").[^45] [^121] [^122] On the other hand, new Game Boys continued to be manufactured and sold.[^123] The console was widespread and, due to its age, affordable to children.[^45] [^124]

Two media channels that would play important roles in the *Pokemon* franchise were the *[CoroCoro Comic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoroCoro_Comic "CoroCoro Comic")*, released monthly, and its sister magazine *Bessatsu CoroCoro Comic*, released bi-monthly. Both [manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga "Manga") magazines are published by [Shogakukan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shogakukan "Shogakukan"), a long-time business partner of Nintendo, and have featured manga based on Nintendo properties (e.g. *[Super Mario-kun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario-kun "Super Mario-kun")*, *[Kirby of the Stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirby_of_the_Stars:_The_Story_of_Dedede_Who_Lives_in_Pupupu "Kirby of the Stars: The Story of Dedede Who Lives in Pupupu")*, *Donkey Kong*). At the time of *Pokemon* 's release, the main *CoroCoro* magazine was read by one in four elementary school students.[^125] *CoroCoro'* s deputy editor-in-chief was [Masakazu Kubo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masakazu_Kubo?action=edit&redlink=1 "Masakazu Kubo (page does not exist)"). On Ishihara's suggestion,[^126] Kubo commissioned the creation of [a manga adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Pocket_Monsters "Pokémon Pocket Monsters"), written and illustrated by [Kosaku Anakubo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kosaku_Anakubo?action=edit&redlink=1 "Kosaku Anakubo (page does not exist)").[^127] Shogakukan, which frequently surveys their target groups, determined that the *Pocket Monsters* manga was well received.[^128]

To further promote *Red* and *Green*, the May issue of *CoroCoro*, released on 15 April 1996, announced the "Legendary Pokemon Offer", centered around a mysterious, secret Pokemon called [Mew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mew_\(Pok%C3%A9mon\) "Mew (Pokémon)").[^129] [^130] Mew was a last-minute addition to *Red & Green*. It is unobtainable in the game(s) through usual means, and was intended to be used at a later point in some post-launch activity.[^131] To participate in the promotion, *CoroCoro* readers had to send in a postcard, and from the entrants, 20 were selected at random. The winners then had to send in their cartridge so that Mew could be uploaded onto it. The lottery was a success and increased [word-of-mouth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-of-mouth_marketing "Word-of-mouth marketing").[^45] [^131] By September, sales of *Red* and *Green* had surpassed 1 million units.[^132]

#### Trading Card Game

The *[Pokemon Trading Card Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pokemon_Trading_Card_Game "Pokemon Trading Card Game")* was one of the first [collectible card games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectible_card_game "Collectible card game") (CCGs) developed in Japan. Its creation was influenced by *[Magic: The Gathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering "Magic: The Gathering")*, the first CCG in history.[^133] [^134] [^135] Indeed, the *Pokemon Trading Card Game* can be considered a simplified version of *Magic*.[^136] [^137] First released in the United States in 1993, *Magic* had gained popularity not just in North America and Europe, but also in Asia.[^138] Ishihara was fond of [playing cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_card "Playing card"),[^100] and had contributed to the development of at least three simple card games designed by [Shigesato Itoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigesato_Itoi "Shigesato Itoi") and released through Ape, Inc.[^7] At the time, Ishihara was particularly interested in *Magic: The Gathering*.[^133] While developing the *Pokemon* RPG, he realized that the concept behind it could be adapted into a *Magic* -like CCG.[^64] The *Pokemon Trading Card Game* was designed by Ishihara,[^145] Akihiko Miura, Kōichi Ōyama, and Takumi Akabane.[^146] All were former staff members of Ape and had previously worked on *[EarthBound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EarthBound "EarthBound")* (1994): Miura was the game's main designer, Ōyama was its art director, and Akabane was one of its chief debuggers.[^147]

While card games [have a long history in Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karuta "Karuta"), a collectible card game was a relatively new concept there, and at the time not widely known.[^148] Because of this, Ishihara had difficulties finding distributors. Sometime in 1995, Ishihara pitched the card game to Nintendo. They agreed to have the cards manufactured, subcontracting an unidentified printing company. However, Nintendo did not want the hassle of having to develop a distribution system from the ground up, i.e. finding retailers willing to sell a CCG.[^149] Ishihara was then contacted by Satoshi Kayama, director of a small firm called [Media Factory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Media_Factory "Media Factory"). Like Ishihara, Kayama was a fan of card games. He felt that CCGs would soon rise to prominence in Japan, and had been gathering information on the possibility of developing such a game in some form. When Kayama heard that Creatures had developed a CCG, he contacted Ishihara and offered to distribute it, signing the contract near the end of 1995.[^150] The first *Pokemon* card set was released on 20 October 1996. Despite being ignored by the media, except for CoroCoro,[^151] the cards became an instant success upon release.[^152]

#### Anime production and premiere

By August 1996, Kubo had become convinced of *Pokemon* 's potential, and believed Shogakukan should create [an anime adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_\(TV_series\) "Pokémon (TV series)").[^153] Nintendo felt reluctant, believing that the property had developed enough as it had over the course of six months. At that time, they did not consider such a significant expansion to be necessary.[^154] Nintendo also noted that if the anime would flop, it would negatively affect future *Pokemon* games.[^155] Ishihara initially opposed the idea, because he thought it would overly hasten the 'consumption' of the property: he feared that if the series would end, people would assume that *Pokemon* has ended, and move on to the next thing.[^156] At the time, Creatures and Game Freak were planning the sequel(s) to *Red* and *Green*, [*Pokemon Gold* and *Silver*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pokemon_Gold_and_Silver "Pokemon Gold and Silver"), and Ishihara did not want the anime to end before they could release their new games.[^157] [^158] Kubo was ultimately able to resolve the concerns of all parties involved. An important aspect of Kubo's bargaining power was the then-ongoing [Mini 4WD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mini_4WD "Mini 4WD") craze and its accompanying hit series *[Bakusō Kyōdai Let's & Go!!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bakus%C5%8D_Ky%C5%8Ddai_Let's_&_Go!! "Bakusō Kyōdai Let's & Go!!")*. Kubo had an important role in the creation of both, which impressed the stakeholders.[^159] To appease Ishihara, Kubo promised him that the anime would last for at least a year and a half. This was unusually long for a debuting anime, and required a big investment.[^157] [^160] Kubo's proposal for *Pocket Monsters* was officially approved on 26 September 1996.[^161] For Nintendo in Japan, it was the first time they licensed a TV series.[^162] Kubo assigned independent producer [Choji Yoshikawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Choji_Yoshikawa?action=edit&redlink=1 "Choji Yoshikawa (page does not exist)") to lead the project.[^163]

[Shogakukan Productions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shogakukan_Productions "Shogakukan Productions"), commonly called ShoPro, was Shogakukan's production company. The animation company they commissioned was [OLM, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OLM,_Inc. "OLM, Inc.") (Oriental Light and Magic), on Kubo's suggestion.[^164] [Kunihiko Yuyama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kunihiko_Yuyama "Kunihiko Yuyama"), one of OLM's founding members, became the anime's director. ShoPro assembled a team of five writers, plus two supporting writers.[^165] All key people involved with the production of *Pokemon* were well-experienced and had proven track records within Japan's anime industry. Yoshikawa felt that the team got lucky. "The probability of so many great people coming together at the same time is very low", he said.[^166] Per Tajiri's explicit condition,[^167] every anime team member had to play the game extensively, including the illustrators and voice actors.[^166] The anime staff "unanimously agreed that the game was interesting" [^168] and "felt connected with each other through the world of *Pokemon* ",[^166] feeling positively challenged to make an anime that would match the game's quality.[^169]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/03/Liam_meets_pikachu_%2814438634188%29.jpg/250px-Liam_meets_pikachu_%2814438634188%29.jpg)

A boy hugging a Pikachu- costumed performer.

A production council was formed to produce the anime. Different people appeared at different meetings, but four individuals usually present were Ishihara of Creatures, Sugimori of Game Freak, Yuyama of OLM, and independent producer Yoshikawa. Yoshikawa had the final say. The council decided on the anime's worldview, characters, general storyline, and various important details. The early meetings, which were also attended by Tajiri, usually started with a Q&A session in which Tajiri and Ishihara were asked about the Pokemon universe.[^170] The council was careful to have the anime be in concordance with the video game. Inevitably, there had to be differences between the two, but all agreed that the overall worldview as envisioned by Tajiri should not be disturbed.[^171] At the start of the video game, the player has to choose one of three starter Pokemon: Fushigidane, Hitokage, or Zenigame ([Bulbasaur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulbasaur "Bulbasaur"), [Charmander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charmander "Charmander"), or [Squirtle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squirtle "Squirtle")). The council didn't want to unfairly popularize any of them, and wanted the protagonist to start out with a different Pokemon.[^172] [^173] They unanimously decided that a [Pikachu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pikachu "Pikachu") should be one of Pokemon's central icons.[^174] They expected Pikachu to appeal to both boys and girls, as well as their mothers. This would expand the franchise's audience, which was considered a core objective of the anime.[^175] [^176]

During the council's first meeting, Yoshikawa brought up the issue on whether the Pokemon in the anime could talk, and if not, how they could communicate. In the video games, each has a specific cry. In [Anakubo's *CoroCoro* manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Pocket_Monsters "Pokémon Pocket Monsters"), most could speak. At first, the council believed there should be a mixture of Pokemon that could talk and some that could not. However, this idea was eventually discarded: the Pokemon had to make a specific cry. The council agreed that Pokemon were like animals, and while they and humans should be able to understand each other in the series, they should not speak each other's language.[^177] It was decided that Pikachu would repeatedly say its own name in various intonations. The role of Pikachu was given to [Ikue Otani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ikue_Otani "Ikue Otani"). During try-outs, Yuyama had Otani voice Pikachu in normal Japanese, as well as in 'Pikachu talk', in which it only said the syllables of its name. Yuyama realized that, even in the latter style of limited communication, Otani was experienced enough to still convey the messages and emotions needed.[^178]

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/ca/Limited-edition_Pok%C3%A9mon-themed_donuts_from_Mister_Donut%2C_Kaohsiung.jpg/250px-Limited-edition_Pok%C3%A9mon-themed_donuts_from_Mister_Donut%2C_Kaohsiung.jpg)

Limited-edition Pokémon-themed donuts from Mister Donut, Kaohsiung

*Pocket Monsters* premiered on 1 April 1997. By November, it had become the highest-rated program on TV Tokyo.[^179]

#### "Dennō Senshi Porygon" incident

In the evening of 16 December 1997, the *Pokemon* franchise was hit by a crisis related to the broadcast of the anime's 38th episode, " [Dennō Senshi Porygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denn%C5%8D_Senshi_Porygon "Dennō Senshi Porygon") " (Computer Warrior Porygon). It was watched by approximately 4.6 million households.[^180] In the episode, the cast is transported into a virtual world, accompanied by a [Porygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porygon "Porygon"), an artificially-made Pokemon. While flying through cyberspace, they are attacked by an anti-virus program which mistakes them for viruses, shooting "vaccine missiles" at them resulting in explosions of bright, rapidly swapping red and blue flashes.[^181]

The intense stimuli brought about by the episode triggered a variety of adverse health effects in more than 10,000 viewers,[^182] primarily irritated eyes, headaches, dizziness, and nausea.[^183] A small part suffered a [photosensitive epileptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photosensitive_epilepsy "Photosensitive epilepsy") [seizure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seizure "Seizure"), manifested in loss of conscious and/or convulsions.[^183] [^184] Hundreds [^8] of children were brought to hospitals, although some had recovered enough upon arrival and did not need to be hospitalized. No one died. Broadcasting of *Pokemon* was halted, and new guidelines were implemented to help prevent similar events from happening.[^190] With the show on [hiatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiatus_\(television\) "Hiatus (television)"), ShoPro and OLM worked on a feature *Pokemon* film. By the time the incident occurred, its script was already written and storyboards were being made. In mid-January, the staff resumed creating new episodes.[^191] The anime series returned on 16 April 1998.[^192] The film, titled *Pocket Monsters the Movie: Mewtwo Strikes Back* (*[Pokémon: The First Movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon:_The_First_Movie "Pokémon: The First Movie")*), premiered on 18 July 1998, becoming the fourth highest grossing film of the year in Japan.[^193] [^194]

Ultimately, the incident did not damage the *Pokemon* franchise – it in fact grew further during and after the anime's hiatus. While video rental tapes were removed from shelves,[^195] [^196] all other *Pokemon* products continued to be sold as usual, and customer demand for them remained high.[^197] [^198] Helping matters was a general understanding among businesses that the anime was not canceled, but rather suspended, and many executives (correctly) expected the show to be resumed after precautions had been taken. Supermarkets and other distribution outlets responded calmly to the crisis, and did not remove *Pokemon* products from their sales floors.[^199]

### 1998–2000: International expansion

> "When we started this project in Japan, one of the first things I was told was that this kind of thing would never appeal to American audiences. They said, 'Because the characters are in a very Japanese style, you cannot sell them to Americans'. So from the very beginning, I never thought there would be an English version. Now, it's just as popular in the United States \[as in Japan\], and I realized that we shouldn't always believe the opinions of conservative marketers."

—  [Shigeru Miyamoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shigeru_Miyamoto "Shigeru Miyamoto"), August 1999 [^200]

Possibly the first official to show interest in a North American launch of *Pokemon* was [Minoru Arakawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minoru_Arakawa "Minoru Arakawa"), founder and then-president of [Nintendo of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_of_America "Nintendo of America") (NoA). Arakawa visited Japan to participate in [Shoshinkai 1996](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Space_World#Shoshinkai_1996 "Nintendo Space World"), held 22–24 November. It was around this time when he first played one of the three *Pokemon* titles released at the time. He thought the games were promising, but Nintendo in Japan (NCL) had no plans at the time to release them elsewhere. He returned to America with a few cartridges and tested the game on his employees – they did not believe it would work in the US.[^201] At the time, [role-playing games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role-playing_video_game "Role-playing video game") (RPGs) were not very popular outside Japan,[^202] and NoA executives believed that American children did not have the [attention span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attention_span "Attention span") for such a complex title.[^203] Americans were said to be more interested in sport- and action-oriented games, preferably with realistic graphics.[^29] [^204] Japanese people, by contrast, were alleged to care more about characters and plot.[^205] Up to that point, few Japanese properties had been successfully mainstreamed in the US, and if they were, it was alleged to be on account of having been properly [Americanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americanized "Americanized"): *[Mighty Morphin Power Rangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mighty_Morphin_Power_Rangers "Mighty Morphin Power Rangers")* was considered a prime example of this.[^206] Visually, *Pokemon* was believed to be too *[kawaii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kawaii "Kawaii")*, or [cute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuteness "Cuteness"). It was assumed that *Pokemon* could not succeed on cute alone – it must also be [cool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cool_\(aesthetic\) "Cool (aesthetic)").[^207] [^208] In an effort to enhance the franchise's coolness, NoA considered a graphical redesign and contracted a few external artists to create some test-designs for the American market.[^203] [^208] The [mockups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mockup "Mockup") they proposed included 'graffiti style' drawings,[^208] 'beefed-up' and more muscular-looking Pokemon,[^209] and a new Pikachu that looked like "a tiger with huge breasts".[^207] Arakawa concluded that it "didn't work",[^208] and by that time, the anime had begun its production in Japan, leading NoA to conclude it was too late for a graphical revamp anyway.[^203]

Of pivotal importance to *Pokemon* 's global expansion was [Alfred R. Kahn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_R._Kahn "Alfred R. Kahn"), CEO of US-based [4Kids Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4Kids_Entertainment "4Kids Entertainment"), NoA's licensing agent since 1987.[^210] [^211] Convinced of the franchise's potential, Kahn agreed to invest an undisclosed sum in return for both the anime and licensing rights.[^202] *Pokemon* became one of the first Japanese media franchises in which both the localization of the anime and the licensing of merchandise was handled by a single company, as well as a non-Japanese company.[^212] Kahn suggested to use the short version of the name, "Pokémon", adding an [acute accent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_accent "Acute accent") (´) over the *e* to assist with pronunciation and "give it a little flair".[^28] NCL president [Hiroshi Yamauchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshi_Yamauchi "Hiroshi Yamauchi") officially approved the project in late November,[^213] and subsequently announced it at [Space World 1997](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Space_World#Space_World_1997 "Nintendo Space World").[^214] However, three weeks later, the " [Dennō Senshi Porygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denn%C5%8D_Senshi_Porygon "Dennō Senshi Porygon") " incident happened, which Kubo felt made even more people resistant to the idea of an overseas introduction.[^215]

[Market research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Market_research "Market research") turned back negative: American kids reportedly did not like *Pokémon*.[^216] Arakawa ignored the study and, convinced of the franchise's potential, allocated an enormous budget to *Pokémon* 's launch. The exact amount was not disclosed, but was reportedly equal to or more than $50 million (c. $98.76 million in 2025), approximately the same amount as the launch budget of the [Nintendo Entertainment System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Entertainment_System "Nintendo Entertainment System") in 1985. Arakawa admitted that it was "quite a bit of money", but NoA "had been doing well for several years, so we had a lot of money to spare". He opined that if *Pokémon* would be as successful in the US as it had been in Japan, "an investment of 1 would turn into 100".[^217] NoA and 4Kids proceeded to plan "an all-out effort to repeat the phenomenon in the Western world".[^45]

The [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editing_of_anime_in_distribution#Localization "Editing of anime in distribution") of the [*Pokémon* anime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_\(TV_series\) "Pokémon (TV series)") was done by 4Kids, and directed by [Norman J. Grossfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_J._Grossfeld "Norman J. Grossfeld"). Grossfeld strongly believed that the anime should be [Americanized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americanization "Americanization").[^218] At [NATPE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NATPE "NATPE") 1998, he asked ShoPro for a "kind of [carte blanche](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/carte%20blanche#Noun "wikt:carte blanche"), to let me change the show as I think would work for this market", to which ShoPro agreed.[^219] However, no national TV station was interested in buying the anime or financing its localization.[^28] [^202] Kahn then decided to self-finance *Pokémon'* s production costs, despite realizing this "could very well bring down 4Kids" if the show would fail.[^219] According to Kahn, they "spend a fortune" on the localization.[^44] To have it [broadcast in syndication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broadcast_syndication "Broadcast syndication"), 4Kids offered the show for free to local TV stations across the country, in exchange for a portion of the advertising revenue.[^28] [^202] NoA assisted, persuading dozens of stations to carry the series by offering to buy some of their advertisement space, spending a total of $5 million (c. $9.88 million in 2025).[^204] [^219] Despite all this, most broadcasters were still reluctant to carry *Pokémon*, with Grossfeld experiencing difficulties getting through their "preconceived notions or their snobbery for Western-produced animation over something from Asia".[^219] At the time, anime had not yet found mainstream popularity in the West. Contemporary news reports cited *[Sailor Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sailor_Moon "Sailor Moon")* [^483] as an example of an anime that had failed to catch on with American youth.[^9] Still, with NoA's help, 4Kids ultimately succeeded in contracting 112 broadcasters for *Pokémon*,[^225] reaching "about 85 to 90 percent" [^226] of television households. However, many broadcasters gave it off-peak time slots, with starting times like 06:00 or 06:30.[^28] [^204] Prior to the late 1990s, this was the case for many anime in the US.[^212]

Grossfeld came up with the [advertising slogan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising_slogan "Advertising slogan") "Gotta catch 'em all!" as the English equivalent to the Japanese *Pokemon GETTO daze~!* (ポケモンゲットだぜー！, Get (the) Pokémon!).[^227] [^228] The phrase "miraculously managed to gain approval" by the [Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission "Federal Communications Commission") (FCC), which normally prohibits the use of injunctions in [ads directed at children](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising_to_children "Advertising to children") (e.g. "You must buy this!"). While the tagline may sound commanding, the FCC reasoned that the act of *catching* is at the core of *Pokémon* 's play. Therefore, the phrase was allowed.[^229] [The series' theme song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Theme "Pokémon Theme") was written by [John Loeffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Loeffler "John Loeffler") and John Siegler, and performed by [Jason Paige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jason_Paige "Jason Paige").[^230]

The *Pokémon* anime was first broadcast on 7 September 1998.[^231] [^232] [*Pokémon Red Version* and *Blue Version*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Red,_Blue,_and_Yellow "Pokémon Red, Blue, and Yellow") were released three weeks later, on 28 September 1998.[^233] To localize the card game, Nintendo contracted [Wizards of the Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wizards_of_the_Coast "Wizards of the Coast"), the creator of *[Magic: The Gathering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic:_The_Gathering "Magic: The Gathering")*.[^234] The *[Pokémon Trading Card Game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Trading_Card_Game "Pokémon Trading Card Game")* was officially launched nationwide on 9 January 1999, although pre-sold in select stores in December.[^235] Coinciding with the North American launch of *Pokémon* was the release of the [Game Boy Color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy_Color "Game Boy Color") on 23 November 1998.[^236]

### 1999–2000: Pokémania

In North America, the debuting *Pokémon* franchise quickly rose to success. By December 1998, the [*Pokémon* anime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_\(TV_series\) "Pokémon (TV series)") had become the highest-rated syndicated children's show during the weekdays.[^237] This attracted the attention of two media companies: [Warner Bros.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warner_Bros. "Warner Bros."), co-owner of [The WB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_WB "The WB") channel; and [Saban Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saban_Entertainment "Saban Entertainment") / [Fox Family Worldwide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Family_Worldwide "Fox Family Worldwide"), owners of the [Fox Kids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Kids "Fox Kids") channel. A bidding war ensued between the parties, which was won by Warner Bros.[^238] On 13 February 1999, *Pokémon* launched on the [Kids' WB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kids'_WB "Kids' WB") national television block. The debut episode became the most watched premiere in Kids' WB's history.[^239]

By April 1999, there was a general consensus in the US that *Pokémon* had become a phenomenon and the newest children's [fad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fad "Fad").[^484] By some, the fad was referred to as "Pokémania",[^485] including journalists of *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\) "Time (magazine)")* [^45] and *[USA Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USA_Today "USA Today")*.[^247] In the US, severe [scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scarcity "Scarcity") occurred of *Pokémon* goods,[^43] especially *Pokémon* cards,[^248] causing companies to miss profits.[^249] A *[Milwaukee Journal Sentinel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milwaukee_Journal_Sentinel "Milwaukee Journal Sentinel")* article, published on 3 August 1999, cited a [Toys "R" Us](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toys_%22R%22_Us "Toys \"R\" Us") manager as saying that a supply of 600 [booster packs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booster_pack "Booster pack") would last 24 hours. The CEO of one card distributor stated that they were "thousands of boxes behind" on orders. In the same article, a Wizards spokeswoman stated that more employees and printers had been hired to increase card production.[^250] However, near the end of the month, a different Wizards spokeswoman told *[The Washington Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Washington_Post "The Washington Post")* that they had "exhausted most of the card-printing capacity of the United States".[^29] Similarly, *USA Today* reported in November 1999 that factories making [Hasbro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasbro "Hasbro") 's *Pokémon* toys had expanded production by 20 times, but demand still exceeded supply.[^251]

In part due to the *Pokémon* craze, Nintendo saw a 250% increase in profits in 1999 compared to the previous year,[^29] reaching a six-year high.[^252] The *Pokémon* franchise accounted for over 30% of Nintendo's revenue that year.[^253] *Pokémon* 's popularity also caused a sharp increase in sales of the Game Boy line.[^203] [^254] [^255] The financial windfalls came at a time when Nintendo lost dominance in the home console market, with the [Nintendo 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_64 "Nintendo 64") being outsold by [Sony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony "Sony") 's [PlayStation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PlayStation_\(console\) "PlayStation (console)").[^256] [^257] [^258] The global success of *Pokémon* compensated this loss somewhat.[^486] Scholars [David Buckingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Buckingham_\(academic\) "David Buckingham (academic)") and Julian Sefton-Green went even further, writing in 2004: "while Nintendo is now among Japan's most profitable corporations, it could be argued that the company would have struggled to survive without Pokémon".[^263]

4Kids, initially a little-known firm,[^202] [^210] expanded thirty times in revenues,[^264] and was named the fastest-growing company in America in the 4 September 2000, issue of *[Fortune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune_\(magazine\) "Fortune (magazine)")* magazine.[^211] [^265] [^266] Many businesses that timely obtained a *Pokémon* license reaped considerable profits.[^264] [^267] In the summer of 1999, a massive run on stocks of publicly traded *Pokémon* licensees caused their value to increase dramatically. However, by November, most investors were [shorting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_\(finance\) "Short (finance)") their shares. Realizing that *Pokémon* was a [fad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fad "Fad") that would peak and fall at some point, investors were [bearish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearish "Bearish") about its prospects.[^268]

*[Pokémon: The First Movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon:_The_First_Movie "Pokémon: The First Movie")* premiered in North America on 12 November 1999, and in Europe the following year. Despite being negatively received by many Western critics, it became one of the most successful Japanese animated films of all time.[^269] In the United States, November 1999 was estimated to have been the peak of Pokémania.[^487] Supporting the American release of *The First Movie* was a promotional action with [Burger King](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burger_King "Burger King"), one of the largest in the history of the [fast-food industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fast_food "Fast food").[^274] [^275] [^276] The success of the promotion resulted in supply issues; restaurants often ran out of *Pokémon* toys to include with their meals.[^277] [^278] On 27 December,[^279] Burger King [recalled its Poké Ball toy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burger_King_Pok%C3%A9mon_container_recall "Burger King Pokémon container recall") after a 13-month-old girl died suffocating on one.[^280] [^281]

[*Pokémon Gold* and *Silver*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Gold,_Silver,_and_Crystal "Pokémon Gold, Silver, and Crystal"), the successors to *Red/Green/Blue*, were released in North America on 15 October 2000.[^282] In Europe, they were released on 6 April 2001.[^283]

#### The Pokémon Company and Pokémon USA established

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/fa/Mega_Tokyo_Pok%C3%A9mon_Center_wares_4.jpg/500px-Mega_Tokyo_Pok%C3%A9mon_Center_wares_4.jpg)

Pokémon Center Mega Tokyo, a large Pokémon merchandise shop in Sunshine City, Ikebukuro. 284

On 23 April 1998, Pokémon Center Co. Ltd. was founded as a joint venture by Nintendo, Creatures, and Game Freak.[^285] It was initially formed for the management of specialized merchandise stores called Pokemon Centers, of which the first location opened in [Nihonbashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nihonbashi "Nihonbashi"), Tokyo, on 18 July 1998.[^286] [^287] Throughout the years, multiple Japanese Pokémon Centers would open and close. As of April 2026, a total of 25 Pokémon merchandise shops exist in Japan.[^288] An American Pokémon Center also existed in New York City from 2001 [^289] to 2005.[^290] It was then remodelled into Nintendo World,[^291] later renamed [Nintendo New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_New_York "Nintendo New York").[^292]

After the release of *Gold* and *Silver*, Tsunekazu Ishihara began setting out a number of long-term goals for the *Pokémon* franchise, which included releasing a movie every year.[^10] As part of Ishihara's plan, the Pokemon Center Co. Ltd. was reformatted into [The Pokémon Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pok%C3%A9mon_Company "The Pokémon Company") (TPC), and officially renamed in October 2000.[^286] [^293] The goal of TPC is to centralize and streamline the global management of Pokémon.[^294] In February 2001, [Pokémon USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_USA "Pokémon USA") was established, an affiliated firm of The Pokémon Company.[^286]

### 2001–2006: End of the craze

In North America, Pokémania peaked in 1999, slowing down throughout the next year.[^295] On 29 April 2000, the anime was bumped off [Kids' WB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kids'_WB "Kids' WB") 's No. 1 spot after holding it for 54 weeks. Around the same time, *Pokémon* was surpassed at [Fox Kids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fox_Kids "Fox Kids") by its rival *[Digimon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digimon_Adventure_\(1999_TV_series\) "Digimon Adventure (1999 TV series)")*.[^296] In Europe, the craze peaked in 2000.[^297] [^298] On 20 January 2001, *The New York Times* reported that *Pokémon* 's trading card market had collapsed in the US.[^299] A June 2001 survey in the United Kingdom confirmed that *Pokémon* 's popularity was waning there.[^300] [Joseph Tobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Tobin "Joseph Tobin") wrote: "By the summer of 2001, Pokémon's shelf space in Japanese and U.S. toy stores was but a fraction of what it enjoyed in the fall of 1999".[^301] By the end of 2001, Pokémania was fading globally, and by 2002, the fad was largely over.[^302]

From 2000 to 2002, Game Freak developed [*Pokémon Ruby* and *Sapphire*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Ruby_and_Sapphire "Pokémon Ruby and Sapphire"), the successors to *Gold* and *Silver*, for the newly released [Game Boy Advance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_Boy_Advance "Game Boy Advance"). Masuda, who was appointed to assistant director during *Gold* and *Silver*,[^303] was promoted to director for *Ruby* and *Sapphire*, with Tajiri making himself executive director.[^304]

In October 2001, [4Kids Entertainment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4Kids_Entertainment "4Kids Entertainment") signed a new contract with [Pokémon USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_USA "Pokémon USA") (PUSA), continuing to serve as *Pokémon* 's exclusive licensing agent and anime localizer.[^294] On 23 December 2005, it was announced that the agreement would not be renewed and would expire on 31 December, with PUSA moving all licensing in-house.[^305] [^306] The localization of the anime would be done by PUSA in cooperation with TAJ Productions. PUSA proceeded to replace almost all of the original English voice actors, who were still under contract with 4Kids.[^307] This decision "raised the ire of fans and the actors themselves".[^308] According to [Stuart Zagnit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Zagnit "Stuart Zagnit"), who voiced [Professor Oak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_Oak "Professor Oak"), the recasting was done to cut back on costs.[^307]

In March 2003, Pokémon UK was established in London as a British representative of [The Pokémon Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pok%C3%A9mon_Company "The Pokémon Company").[^286]

### 2006–2012: Generation IV–V, TPCi established

Following *Ruby and Sapphire*, [*Pokémon Diamond* and *Pearl*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Diamond_and_Pearl "Pokémon Diamond and Pearl") were released for the [Nintendo DS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_DS "Nintendo DS") on 28 September 2006, in Japan [^309] and on 22 April 2007, in North America.[^310] A third version, *[Pokémon Platinum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Platinum "Pokémon Platinum")*, was released on 28 September 2008, in Japan [^311] and on 22 March 2009, in North America.[^312] They form the fourth generation (Generation IV) in the *Pokémon* video game series.[^313] *Diamond and Pearl* were designed based on the DS's various features such as its Wi-Fi capabilities and slot for Game Boy Advance cartridge.[^314] Pokémon president Tsunekazu Ishihara dubbed the games as the "ultimate" Pokémon titles because they allowed the player to trade and battle Pokémon including every Pokémon from previous iterations globally through WiFi, as opposed to previously only able to do so locally and with fewer Pokémon.[^315] The games' characters are 2D and the environments are rendered in 3D, and it is considerably difficult to differentiate them because Game Freak designed them this way to innovate the graphics while also retaining the traditional game style and feel.[^316] [^317]

In 2009, Pokémon USA and Pokémon UK merged to form [The Pokémon Company International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pok%C3%A9mon_Company_International "The Pokémon Company International") (TPCi).[^26] This subsidiary of [The Pokémon Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pok%C3%A9mon_Company "The Pokémon Company") (TPC) has since managed the *Pokémon* franchise outside of Asia.[^27]

In Generation V, [*Pokémon Black* and *White*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Black_and_White "Pokémon Black and White") were released on 18 September 2010, in Japan for the DS [^318] and on 6 March 2011, in North America.[^319] The games feature enhanced visual effects and increased use of 3D graphics. The developers excluded old Pokémon and strategies, while introducing over 150 new Pokémon to evoke a sense of novelty and to provide new players a more "leveled playing field" against old players.[^320] The games seek to attract both new players and returning players through its detailed walkthrough and the addition of C-Gear (a real-time communication tool to improve the trade and battle experiences), respectively.[^321] *Black* and *White* were followed by [*Pokémon Black 2* and *White 2*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Black_2_and_White_2 "Pokémon Black 2 and White 2")*,* which were released on 23 June 2012, in Japan [^322] and on 7 October 2012, in North America.[^323]

### 2013–2018: 3D, Generation VI–VII, Pokémon Go

In Generation VI, [*Pokémon X* and *Y*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_X_and_Y "Pokémon X and Y") were released worldwide for the 3DS on 12 October 2013,[^324] and they are the first games to be released in this way. The developers focused on the themes of "beauty", "bond", and "evolution", and it is based on these themes that they created the games' core mechanic where Pokémon could achieve a higher form of evolution by strengthening their bonds with trainers; however, to maintain game balance, they limited this feature to a special, temporary phenomenon.[^325] The games' shift to fully 3D graphics allowed the player to freely manipulate the camera angles although due to technical limitations objects far away are rendered in lower [polygon models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygonal_modeling "Polygonal modeling"), and producer Hitoshi Yamagami addressed to 2D players that "Game Freak \[developers are also\] big fans of 2D graphics... So there are some elements in there that I \[Yamagami\] think fans of 2D will appreciate as well." [^326] When asked about the Horde battle and Sky battle types, he stated that Horde battles are to supplant the roles of "really strong pokemon \[*[sic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sic "Sic")*\]" to help the player more easily progress through the games and also to add "more excitement to the battles and a sense of danger", and that they added the Sky battles to take advantage of the games' 3D camera angels capabilities.[^327]

In Generation VII, [*Pokémon Sun* and *Moon*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Sun_and_Moon "Pokémon Sun and Moon") were released worldwide for the 3DS on 18 November 2016, and on 23 November 2016, in Europe.[^328] The developers chose Hawaii for inspiration due to its distinctive warm sunlight and clear moonlight and unique biomes that helped support the games' regional Pokémon variance concept. Ohmori said, as director, he aimed to focus on the concept of "Pokémon as these living creatures, \[and really focus\] on them being alive." Having the release date on the 20th anniversary of Pokémon in mind, he treated this project as a "celebration of that life, and to really express this respect for life". As he contemplated about life and its origins, Ohmori considered the significance of the Sun, enabling life with its [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunlight "Sunlight"), and the Moon's [impact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_effect "Lunar effect") on certain species' reproduction. He explained how the Sun, the Moon, and the Earth "work \[together\] to influence \[one another\], and life as a result grows and flourishes based on \[this\] relationship." [^329]

#### 2016: Release of Pokémon Go

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/e2/Pokemon_go_fest.jpg/250px-Pokemon_go_fest.jpg)

Players during the Pokémon Go Fest in Chicago in 2017

In 2016, the *Pokémon* franchise spawned a second worldwide fad with the release of *[Pokémon Go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Go "Pokémon Go")*, a mobile [augmented reality game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmented_reality_game "Augmented reality game"). The app originated as a [Google April Fools' Day joke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Google_April_Fools'_Day_jokes "List of Google April Fools' Day jokes") in 2014: the " [Google Maps Pokémon Challenge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Google_April_Fools'_Day_jokes#Google_Maps_Pok%C3%A9mon_Challenge "List of Google April Fools' Day jokes") ". The prank was conceived by [Tsunekazu Ishihara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsunekazu_Ishihara "Tsunekazu Ishihara") and [Satoru Iwata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satoru_Iwata "Satoru Iwata").[^330] On Ishihara's initiation,[^331] the hoax was turned into an actual video game developed by [Niantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niantic,_Inc. "Niantic, Inc."). Ishihara was a fan of Niantic's previous [transreality game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transreality_game "Transreality game"), *[Ingress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingress_\(video_game\) "Ingress (video game)")*, and saw the game's concept as a perfect match for *Pokémon*.[^331]

Through in-game purchases, the game generated more than $160 million by the end of July 2016,[^332] with App Annie reporting that *Pokémon Go* had generated around $10 million in revenue every day that month.[^333] The same month, Sensor Tower reported that the game had passed more than $200 million in worldwide revenue, beating every existing record set by *[Clash of Clans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clash_of_Clans "Clash of Clans")* and *[Candy Crush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candy_Crush "Candy Crush")* by a wide margin.[^334] The average daily usage of the app on Android devices in July 2016 exceeded that of [Snapchat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snapchat "Snapchat"), [Tinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tinder_\(app\) "Tinder (app)"), Twitter, [Instagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instagram "Instagram"), and Facebook.[^335] By 2 September 2016, *Pokémon Go* had generated more than $440 million in worldwide revenue, according to Sensor Tower.[^336] By 30 September, it had received 500 million downloads and grossed $470 million in 80 days, according to market research firm Newzoo.[^337] *Pokémon Go* reached the milestone of $600 million in revenue after only 90 days on the market, becoming the fastest mobile game ever to do so.[^338]

### 2019–present: Generation VIII–IX, open-world gameplay

Generation VIII [^339] debuted with the release of [*Pokémon Sword* and *Shield*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Sword_and_Shield "Pokémon Sword and Shield") on 15 November 2019, for the Switch.[^340] Director Shigeru Ohmori stated that they designed the games based on what they believed of the biggest *Pokémon* theme of becoming/being "the greatest or strongest", which was expressed in the games' [Dynamax / Gigantamax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_\(video_game_series\)#2019%E2%80%932022:_Eighth_generation "Pokémon (video game series)") Pokémon core mechanic and the games' increasingly powerful software and hardware capabilities. Ohmori further revealed that through developing the *Let's Go* games as research projects for the Switch, they were able to gain valuable experiences and knowledge to develop *Sword* and *Shield*. He noted that they took advantage of the Switch's high resolution and TV connectivity to implement the games' gigantic-size core Pokémon mechanic. They envisioned the games' setting to be a "wide-open space" that is different from the traditional route systems and is constantly changing where the player can meet and explore with other players.[^341]

In 2020, the [COVID-19 pandemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic "COVID-19 pandemic") broke out. The resulting widespread ennui and excessive leisure time inspired a resurgence in popularity and interest of *Pokémon* cards,[^342] which was further popularized by various [YouTubers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YouTuber "YouTuber") and other [influencers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Influencer "Influencer"), such as [Logan Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logan_Paul "Logan Paul").[^343] [^344] The craze resulted in severe supply shortages, and customers' inappropriate and obsessive behavior raised safety concerns in many retail outlets. *Pokémon* [cards' values skyrocketed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_most_expensive_CCG_cards "List of most expensive CCG cards"), prompting collectors to submit cards and overwhelm [card grading agencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trading_card#Condition "Trading card").[^345] [^346] The Pokémon Company responded by reprinting impacted products at maximum capacity to ensure price stabilization and general accessibility.[^347]

*[Pokémon Legends: Arceus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Legends:_Arceus "Pokémon Legends: Arceus")* was released on 28 January 2022, for the Switch as a prequel to *Diamond* and *Pearl*.[^348] It is part of Generation VIII.[^349] The game "represents a new approach for the *Pokémon* video game series" as it transitions from traditional RPG mechanics to add real-time elements in their games. Unlike traditional *Pokémon* games, the player can catch Pokémon by throwing a Poké Ball in real-time rather than triggering a battle; however, they can still choose the latter to weaken it for capture. Many in-game aspects, particularly its landscape, heavily resemble those of *[The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Zelda:_Breath_of_the_Wild "The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild")*.[^350] [^351] [^352]

Generation IX [^353] was introduced with the release of [*Pokémon Scarlet* and *Violet*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Scarlet_and_Violet "Pokémon Scarlet and Violet") for the Switch on 18 November 2022. The games are the first *Pokémon* games to feature an entirely open-world experience as opposed to the partially open-world experience in previous *Pokémon* games such as *Sword and Shield,* and it also departed from the traditional gym mechanics and the elite four battles to opt for unique road quests. The games' improved multiplayer experience allowed for up to four players to travel together.[^354] [^355] Their DLC, [*The Hidden Treasure of Area Zero*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Scarlet_and_Violet:_The_Hidden_Treasure_of_Area_Zero "Pokémon Scarlet and Violet: The Hidden Treasure of Area Zero"), consists of two parts, *The Teal Mask* and *The Indigo Disk*; *The Teal Mask* was released on 12–13 September 2023 [^356] and *The Indigo Disk* was released on 14 December 2023.[^357] An epilogue to *The Hidden Treasure of Area Zero* was released on 11 January 2024.[^358]

*[Pokémon Legends: Z-A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Legends:_Z-A "Pokémon Legends: Z-A")* was released on 16 October 2025 for the [Nintendo Switch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Switch "Nintendo Switch") and [Nintendo Switch 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Switch_2 "Nintendo Switch 2").[^359] [^360] It is part of Generation IX. A successor to *Legends: Arceus*, this game returns to the Kalos region seen in *X* and *Y*. A DLC, *Mega Dimension*, released on 10 December 2025.[^361] The official logo for the 30th anniversary of the franchise was officially unveiled by the official social media accounts on the new year of 2026.[^362]

In January 2026, [Lego](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lego "Lego") announced their first ever *Pokémon* sets available for preorder to be released February 27, 2026.[^363] According to [Yahoo News](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yahoo_News "Yahoo News"), their massive $650 set sold out quickly and were already being resold on [eBay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EBay "EBay") before their official release.[^364]

On 27 February 2026, the franchises 30th anniversary, *[Pokémon Winds and Waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Winds_and_Waves "Pokémon Winds and Waves")* were announced in a [*Pokémon* Presents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo_Direct "Nintendo Direct") live stream as the first Generation X games. The games are set to release in 2027.

## Reaction to Pokémania (1999–2000)

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/en/thumb/1/18/Pikachu_on_New_Yorker_cover%2C_Nov_1_1999.jpg/250px-Pikachu_on_New_Yorker_cover%2C_Nov_1_1999.jpg)

1 November 1999 cover of The New Yorker, which depicts a Pikachu carrying away a bag overflowing with money while trick-or-treating. Artwork by Harry Bliss.

In 1999 and 2000, *Pokémon* was an unprecedented, ubiquitous [fad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fad "Fad") in the Western world. *[Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_\(magazine\) "Time (magazine)")* magazine described it as "a multimedia and interactive barrage like no other before it".[^45] The franchise, primarily aimed at children, elicited mixed responses from parents and teachers, some of them critical. In a 2004 essay, anthropologist Christine R. Yano even claimed that the reactions at one point constituted a [moral panic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moral_panic "Moral panic").[^383]

The bulk of the criticism on *Pokémon* was directed at [the trading cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_Trading_Card_Game "Pokémon Trading Card Game"),[^384] in particular the [booster packs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booster_pack "Booster pack"), sealed packages of 11 [^11] randomly inserted cards that were sold separately from the main sets. The cards are of varying scarcity, the most valuable being the "holofoil cards" (also called "holographic" or "foil cards"), in which the illustrations of the Pokémon have a shiny overlay effect. The rare cards can only be found in booster packs, and the rarest ones are very infrequently included. [Joseph Tobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Tobin "Joseph Tobin") noted that rarity in this case is "artificially created", and "effectively a form of gambling" in which children need to repeatedly purchase booster packs to get more rare cards.[^385] [Gilles Brougère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gilles_Broug%C3%A8re?action=edit&redlink=1 "Gilles Brougère (page does not exist)") described a cynicism among adults that corporations could apparently, "out of thin air", ascribe value to cards which they saw as valueless, thereby "deceiving vulnerable young consumers and garnering excessive profits".[^384]

As the franchise's popularity grew, children began taking their *Pokémon* cards to school for trading and playing. Soon, the cards were alleged to be "disrupting learning, poisoning playground friendships and causing such distraction that some children forget their homework, tune out in class and even miss school buses as they scramble to acquire one more card".[^386] The cards were "turning the playground into a black market",[^387] with card swaps sometimes inciting conflicts. Certain children engaged in "aggressive trading",[^388] tricking other (often younger) kids into unfair deals, forcing teachers to arbitrate.[^488] Some parents expressed their concerns about the craze, but feared that their children would be ostracized if they were to deny them *Pokémon* products.[^388] [^393] [^394] In the US, the *Pokémon* cards ended up "almost universally banned" from school grounds.[^395] Similar bans occurred in Canada,[^396] [^397] Australia,[^398] New Zealand,[^399] and European countries.[^388] [^400] [^401] In September 1999, US-based law firm [Milberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milberg "Milberg") filed a [class-action lawsuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class-action_lawsuit "Class-action lawsuit"), claiming that the booster packs constitute a form of lottery and promote gambling in kids.[^402] [^403] The suit is believed to have been settled.[^12]

The [*Pokémon* anime series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_\(TV_series\) "Pokémon (TV series)") was criticized by some as "cheap Japanese animation" [^405] that is "violent" [^46] [^406] [^407] and has "little educational value".[^408] Michelle Orecklin of *Time* dismissed the TV series as "less a cartoon than a half-hour exercise in Pokémon product placement".[^409] [Anne Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Allison "Anne Allison") wrote that even those within Pokémon's US marketing team agreed that the anime's visuals were "not especially sophisticated" compared to [Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney "Disney") cartoons.[^405] *[Pokémon: The First Movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon:_The_First_Movie "Pokémon: The First Movie")* premiered in the United States on 12 November 1999, and in European countries in February 2000. While a huge box-office success, the film was received negatively by several Western film critics.[^410] [^411] *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian "The Guardian")* decried it as a "contemptuously cheap animated cash-in on the monster kids' craze".[^412] The American adult animated series *[South Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Park "South Park")* satirized *Pokémon* in the episode " [Chinpokomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinpokomon "Chinpokomon") ", aired on 3 November 1999. In the episode, the titular media franchise is portrayed as a low-quality line of products that is part of an evil plan by the Japanese government to invade the US. *South Park* co-creator [Matt Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matt_Stone "Matt Stone") commented that, at the time, *Pokémon* was "scary huge".[^413]

Author Chris Kohler wrote that *Pokémon* was considered "ruthlessly commercial", and that it "program\[med\] children to be consumers of anything and everything Pokémon".[^414] [CNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNN "CNN") quoted child psychiatrist John Lochridge as worrying that "Pokémon's creators and marketers deliberately set out to create a fantasy world so compelling that children would quickly become obsessed". He believed that kids were being "brainwashed", and said: "I have had parents tell me that they cannot get their kids to do anything except Pokémon, so this stuff seems to really capture their minds, in a way".[^19] These concerns were countered by psychologist [William Damon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Damon "William Damon"), who told *[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek "Newsweek")* that obsessing is in fact a normal part of a child's neurological development. It should concern parents only when the obsession gets dangerous or excessive.[^415] An [op-ed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op-ed "Op-ed") in the New Zealander newspaper *[The Dominion Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dominion_Post_\(Wellington\) "The Dominion Post (Wellington)")* claimed that the anti-Pokémon sentiment was particularly American: "The backlash, which seems largely confined to the United States, may be no more than the sound of the world's leading cultural imperialist gagging on a taste of its own medicine".[^416]

As Pokémania built, Western media started reporting on several crimes associated with *Pokémon*. These included violence [^398] [^417] (including two reported stabbings),[^418] [^419] burglaries,[^420] [^421] robberies [^400] [^417] (some at knifepoint),[^422] [^423] and shoplifting.[^424] [^425] Almost all these incidents were connected to the *Pokémon* cards, and the individuals involved were almost always underage. In the US, the incidents peaked in November 1999.[^270] In England, *Pokémon* -related delinquency reached a head in April 2000.[^422] [^426] High prices on the [grey market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grey_market "Grey market") were a motive behind some of the crimes, "posing a great temptation for older kids and bullies to take advantage of weaker children".[^427] At specialty shops and online auctions, a rare *Pokémon* card could be bought and sold for $50 or more (c. $97 in 2025).[^428] [^429]

Aside from the negative reactions, many media also cited alleged beneficial effects of *Pokémon*.[^430] [^431] [^432] It was noted that the video games and the cards require children to read, memorize, calculate, and plan out a strategy.[^18] [^152] [^433] Both encourage socialization, and trading Pokémon requires negotiating skills.[^19] [^29] [^41] [Stephanie Strom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephanie_Strom "Stephanie Strom") wrote in *[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")* that the *Pokémon* anime taught children "traditional Japanese values – responsibility, empathy, cooperation, obedience, respect for elders, humility".[^434] [Anne Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Allison "Anne Allison") interviewed various American parents during Pokémania. She found that while most of them were "utterly mystified" about *Pokémon*, few were overly worried about it, instead meeting the craze with "befuddled acceptance". Allison also notes that the [Columbine High School massacre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbine_High_School_massacre "Columbine High School massacre") occurred during Pokémania (on 20 April 1999), causing violent television, music, and games to be scrutinized. Compared to these media, she notes, *Pokémon* is in fact rather tame.[^435]

Pokemon's rapid rise in popularity resulted in popular conspiracy theories as well as significant governmental and religious scrutiny in the Muslim world. In 2001, Sheikh [Abdulaziz bin Abdullah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abdulaziz_Al_Sheikh "Abdulaziz Al Sheikh"), the [Grand Mufti of Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Mufti_of_Saudi_Arabia "Grand Mufti of Saudi Arabia"), issued a fatwa banning Pokemon in Saudi Arabia on the basis that it promotes gambling and that the cards contain symbols resembling "the star of David, which everyone knows is connected to international Zionism and is Israel's national emblem". He also claimed that other symbols shown on the card promoted Freemasonry and Christianity.[^436] As a result of this edict, Pokemon products were removed from shops in Saudi Arabia and customs officers began preventing them from entering the country. [^437] Various conspiracy theories circulated in the Arab World about Jewish themes in Pokemon, and Japanese embassies throughout the region received inquiries from local officials who were concerned that "Pokemon" meant "I am a Jew" in Japanese. In Jordan, Sheikh Abdel Monem abu Zent, a former member of parliament, claimed that Pokemon "is part of a Jewish plan to corrupt the mind of our young generation because it alludes to blasphemous thinking, it mocks our God and our moral values".[^438]

## Legacy and influences

After [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II"), Japan experienced [a period of unprecedented growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_economic_miracle "Japanese economic miracle") and became well-known in the Western world for its consumer products, such as [radios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor_radio#Japanese_transistor_radios "Transistor radio") (e.g. [Panasonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panasonic "Panasonic"), [Toshiba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toshiba "Toshiba")), [cars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automotive_industry_in_Japan "Automotive industry in Japan") (e.g. [Toyota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toyota "Toyota"), [Mitsubishi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi "Mitsubishi")), and [Sony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sony "Sony") 's [Walkman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walkman "Walkman").[^439] From 1991 onwards, [its economy stagnated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_Decades "Lost Decades"), causing the country to lose its status as an economic superpower. However, during the 1990s and 2000s, Japan re-emerged as a source of 'cool' cultural goods, embraced by a growing international audience interested in [Japanese culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_culture "Japanese culture").[^489] [Nintendo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nintendo "Nintendo"), [Sega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sega "Sega"), and Sony launched several popular video game consoles and franchises.[^205] [^444] [^445] In terms of children's properties, the success of *[Mighty Morphin Power Rangers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mighty_Morphin_Power_Rangers "Mighty Morphin Power Rangers")* changed perceptions on the viability of such Japanese imports in the West.[^446] [^447] [^448] The [Tamagotchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamagotchi "Tamagotchi") fad (1997–98),[^449] centered around a portable [digital pet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_pet "Digital pet") device, was said to have "paved the way for Pokémon".[^204]

[Anne Allison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Allison "Anne Allison") wrote that, before the 1990s, Japan figured little in the face of the worldwide hegemony of Euro-American cultural industries, in particular that of the US. "Hollywood has been hostile to imports", she wrote, "and foreignness has largely been, and been seen as, an impediment to mass popularization in the United States".[^450] The surprise success of *Pokémon* was "an undeniable breakthrough in the homeland of [Disney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney "Disney") " that "changed preexisting assumptions about the US marketplace at the same time that it was constantly resisted for deviating from them".[^451] *Pokémon* was a welcomed boon to Japan's faltering economy,[^452] and positively influenced the country's [soft power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_power "Soft power").[^453] [^454] Sociologist Yoshinori Kamo interviewed various American children and found that kids who thought *Pokémon* was cool, were more likely to believe that Japan was a cool nation.[^455] Anne Allison gave a similar finding: all the children she interviewed knew where *Pokémon* originated, and "many said that, as a result of *Pokémon* and other 'cool' Japanese goods, they had developed an interest in Japan. A number said that they now wanted to study Japanese and travel there one day".[^456] Author Chris Kohler wrote: "Japanese are proud of *Pokémon*, the most successful export of Japanese popular culture ever".[^457] Although, sociologist Koichi Iwabuchi questioned to what extent *Pokémon* really is 'Japanese', and to what extent it is simply a good property with universal appeal. He noted that [Japanese nationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_nationalist "Japanese nationalist") commentators celebrated *Pokémon* 's global success and retrospectively attributed this to its "Japanese cultural power",[^458] while ignoring the localization of *Pokémon* overseas,[^459] as well as decades of increasing cooperation and cultural exchange between countries ([globalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globalization "Globalization")).[^460]

In the 20th century, [anime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anime "Anime") found niche popularity in North America and Europe in series (*[Astro Boy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astro_Boy "Astro Boy")*, *[Kimba the White Lion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimba_the_White_Lion "Kimba the White Lion")*, *[Speed Racer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_Racer "Speed Racer")*) and films (*[Akira](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_\(1988_film\) "Akira (1988 film)")*, *[Ghost in the Shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_in_the_Shell_\(1995_film\) "Ghost in the Shell (1995 film)")*).[^490] The [*Pokémon* TV series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon_\(TV_series\) "Pokémon (TV series)") and [films](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Pok%C3%A9mon_films "List of Pokémon films") marked a breakthrough for anime, contributing to its growing worldwide success at the turn of the 21st century.[^491] For some children, *Pokémon* was their introduction to 'Japanimation',[^465] [^466] serving as a "gateway" to other anime, manga, and Japanese culture in general.[^467] [^468] *[Pokémon: The First Movie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pok%C3%A9mon:_The_First_Movie "Pokémon: The First Movie")* became one of the most successful Japanese animated films in history.[^269] After *[Princess Mononoke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princess_Mononoke "Princess Mononoke")*, *Pokémon: The First Movie* became the second anime film to open at mainstream cinemas in the West, as opposed to the usual art house venues.[^469] Pioneering anime importer [John Ledford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Ledford "John Ledford") noted that *Pokémon* underscored the commercial potential of anime, thus making it interesting from a business perspective.[^470]

The success of *Pokémon* encouraged companies to look for other popular Japanese properties that might be localized for Western markets.[^212] [^471] [^472] The importing of at least three similar franchises were confirmed by business executives to have been (partly) inspired by *Pokémon*: *[Yu-Gi-Oh!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yu-Gi-Oh! "Yu-Gi-Oh!")*,[^473] *[Digimon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digimon "Digimon")*,[^474] and *[Monster Rancher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monster_Rancher "Monster Rancher")*.[^475] The import of *[Cardcaptor Sakura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardcaptor_Sakura "Cardcaptor Sakura")* (as *Cardcaptors*) might also have been prompted by *Pokémon*.[^476] [^477]
