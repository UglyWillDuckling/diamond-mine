### About 'Taro the Dragon Boy.'

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!wne5!,w_424,c_limit,f_webp,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fc356875f-505d-4e33-8d0b-41ee7dd779af_1918x871.png)

A still from Taro the Dragon Boy (1979)

**Welcome!** This is a new Thursday edition of the *Animation Obsessive* newsletter — all about an underrated film from Japan.

Back in the ‘60s, [[Isao Takahata]] was a young, rising talent at [[Toei Doga]], the most renowned animation studio in Tokyo. He worked as an assistant director on its *[Anju and Zushiomaru](https://x.com/ani_obsessive/status/1941204989449470179)* (1961) and *[Little Prince and the Eight-Headed Dragon](https://x.com/ani_obsessive/status/1666395296761298947)* (1963). With the TV cartoon *Wolf Boy Ken*, he started to direct himself.

Around ‘65, he moved up in a big way. A senior animator named [[Yasuo Otsuka]] wanted to collaborate with him on a feature. The two had grown close; both were active in Toei Doga’s labor union. So, Otsuka battled management until Takahata got his own movie to direct.[1](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-spirit-of-horus#footnote-1-178056930)

From there, a project took shape through meetings between Takahata and Otsuka, plus a few others. Their source material was a ‘60s novel for children, *[[Taro The Dragon Boy (1979)]]*, based on Japanese folklore. Toei Doga had batted around the idea of animating it for a few years. Seemingly, Takahata finished at least *part* of a screenplay, and figured out some of the characters.[2](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-spirit-of-horus#footnote-2-178056930)

This *Taro* project was shelved at an “early” stage, remembered Otsuka. Among other concerns, the story lacked the “scale” they wanted — the novel isn’t an epic. Takahata and his team took a different route and, ultimately, created *Horus: Prince of the Sun* (1968) instead. That film changed animation in Japan for good.

Takahata, Otsuka and many of their friends quit Toei over the next few years. In the ‘70s, they made *[Lupin the 3rd](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-real-lupin)*, *[Panda! Go, Panda!](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/totoro-before-totoro)* and *[Heidi](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-pressure-that-made-miyazaki-and)* elsewhere. Takahata especially had little choice but to leave. He’d burned bridges at Toei — as a top union member and as the head of *Horus*, which went over-schedule and initially bombed. “\[T\]he company saw him as their foe,” Hayao Miyazaki explained.[3](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-spirit-of-horus#footnote-3-178056930)

But the studio didn’t forget Takahata’s plan for *Taro the Dragon Boy*. During the late ‘70s, it was revived. Toei Doga’s reputation had faded a bit in the meantime: lots of its best artists were gone, and the remaining team did faster, less polished, less ambitious work. *Taro* was meant to be a return.[4](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-spirit-of-horus#footnote-4-178056930)

According to a higher-up at the company, the hope was to make *Taro* a “culmination of Toei Doga’s 22 years.” There’s an argument that it was. The anime critic Ben Ettinger wrote that *Taro* was the studio’s “last great film.” [5](https://animationobsessive.substack.com/p/the-spirit-of-horus#footnote-5-178056930)
